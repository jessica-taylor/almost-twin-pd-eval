# Choosing Policies, Not Actions: An Introduction to Policy Functional Decision Theory (P-FDT)

## 1. Introduction

Suppose you are standing in front of two boxes, deciding whether to take one or both. Causal decision theory (CDT) and evidential decision theory (EDT) frame this as a question about which *act* to perform: which physical motion of your body is best, or whose occurrence would be best news. The functional decision theory literature (Yudkowsky and Soares, "Functional Decision Theory: A New Theory of Instrumental Rationality," arXiv:1710.05060) argues that both framings are subtly wrong. The right question, they claim, is: *which output of my decision procedure yields the best outcome?* — evaluating your decision as the output of a fixed mathematical function, and considering counterfactuals in which that function's output differs.

In this essay I focus on a refinement that the FDT paper mentions only in a footnote, but which its authors identify as their preferred formulation. Footnote 16 says:

> "As mentioned earlier, the author's preferred formulation of FDT actually intervenes on the node FDT(−) to choose not an action but a *policy* which maps inputs to actions, to which the agent then applies her inputs in order to select an action. The difference only matters in multi-agent dilemmas so far as we can tell, so we have set that distinction aside in this paper for ease of exposition."

I will call this the **policy-selection formulation**, and the resulting decision theory **policy functional decision theory (P-FDT)**. P-FDT agrees with action-FDT in the paper's central single-agent dilemmas, but it makes a structural change with philosophical significance of its own: the unit of evaluation is not "what should I do now?" but "what should my response function be?" — where a response function maps every observation history you might receive to an action you would take on receiving it. This essay develops P-FDT mathematically, shows how it works on the classic dilemmas, explains where and why the policy-level formulation matters, and flags the points where the source leaves important choices unresolved.

## 2. Background: hypotheticals as the crux of decision theory

All three theories at issue are expected utility theories. Following the source paper, an agent with observations $x$ chooses an action $a$ maximizing

$$
\mathcal{EU}(a) = \sum_{j} P(a \hookrightarrow o_j \,;\, x)\,\mathcal{U}(o_j),
$$

where $P(a \hookrightarrow o_j\,;\,x)$ is the agent's probability that outcome $o_j$ obtains *in the hypothetical scenario where* $a$ is executed after observations $x$. The theories disagree about the interpretation of "$\hookrightarrow$", the decision-hypothetical connective. Joyce's representation theorem guarantees that any coherent agent wants to maximize expected utility *relative to some constraints* on these hypotheticals; the theories are competing accounts of the "epistemic perspective from which actions are to be evaluated."

Concretely, each theory maintains a world-model $M$ and, for each candidate act $a$, constructs a hypothetical $M^{a\hookrightarrow}$ by performing surgery on $M$ that preserves some correlations and breaks others:

- **EDT** conditions: $M^{a\hookrightarrow}$ is $P(- \mid x, a)$. All correlations between the act and the world persist, including merely statistical ones. This makes EDT agents "manage the news": in the smoking lesion problem they forgo smoking because smoking is *evidence* of the lesion, and in the XOR blackmail problem they pay a blackmailer because paying is evidence their house is termite-free.
- **CDT** intervenes causally: $M^{a\hookrightarrow}$ is $P(- \mid \mathrm{do}(\mathrm{Act}=a))$, following Pearl. Correlations caused by the act are preserved; everything else is severed. This fixes the smoking lesion but breaks too much: in Newcomb's problem the CDT agent imagines her action changing while the predictor's (already-made, accurate) prediction stays fixed — imagining a perfect predictor erring.
- **FDT** intervenes on a *logical* variable: the node representing the output of the agent's decision function on the current inputs. Everything that subjunctively depends on that output — the agent's action, the predictor's prediction, a twin's symmetric decision — varies with the intervention; everything else is held fixed. Formally, given a distribution $P$ augmented with a graph $G$ of subjunctive-dependence links:

$$
\mathrm{FDT}(P, G, x) = \arg\max_{a}\, \mathbb{E}\big[V \mid \mathrm{do}\big(\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x}) = a\big)\big].
$$

Here $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G},\underline{x})$ is a *variable in the model* representing what the FDT algorithm outputs on inputs $(P,G,x)$ — a self-reference that is harmless by Kleene's recursion theorem ("quining"). FDT agents one-box in Newcomb's problem, smoke in the smoking lesion problem, pay Parfit's hitchhiker, and refuse blackmail — all from one rule.

The counterfactuals involved are *counterpossibles*: since a fixed mathematical function provably has a unique output on a fixed input, "what if my decision function output differently?" has a logically impossible antecedent. The paper treats this as a technical rather than philosophical difficulty, requiring that the structure of subjunctive dependencies be supplied as input (just as causal structure is supplied as input to CDT), and notes that constructing that structure from observation — the analogue of Pearl's causal discovery — is the theory's largest open problem.

## 3. From actions to policies: the P-FDT move

The formalization above intervenes on $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$ — the function's output *at the current observation* $x$. Footnote 16 (and footnote 7, which says agents "actually iterate over *policies* (mappings from observations to actions) rather than actions") proposes intervening one level higher.

### 3.1 Definitions

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be the set of possible observation histories and $\mathcal{A}$ a finite action set. A **policy** is a function $\pi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{A}$. Write $\Pi$ for the set of policies. The agent's decision algorithm, viewed as a function of its inputs, induces a policy $\pi^\ast : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{A}$; the P-FDT agent intervenes on a logical variable

$$
\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P}, \underline{G}) \;=\; \pi^\ast,
$$

representing the *entire input–output map* of her decision procedure, rather than its value at $x$. The prescription is:

$$
\boxed{\;\mathrm{P\text{-}FDT}(P, G) \;=\; \arg\max_{\pi \in \Pi}\; \mathbb{E}\big[V \mid \mathrm{do}\big(\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G}) = \pi\big)\big],\;}
$$

and the agent then **executes** $\pi(x)$ for her actual observation $x$. (As in the source, the expectation is taken with respect to the modified distribution $P$ after the intervention, in which all variables subjunctively depending on $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G})$ — including any predictors modeling the agent, twins sharing her decision function, and her own future actions — update to reflect policy $\pi$.)

Two remarks on this definition:

1. **The argmax is over the full policy space, but the intervention is on one variable.** There is a potential ambiguity here that the footnote does not resolve: does the agent consider a hypothetical in which *only her current decision point* runs policy $\pi$ while the rest of her algorithm (and hence all things computing "the same function") is unchanged, or does the intervention change the *whole* function $\pi^\ast$ everywhere it is implemented or predicted? The latter reading is the only coherent one in the spirit of subjunctive dependence: the variable $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G})$ is a *type*, not a token (recall the paper's Peirce-style point that CDT intervenes on the token "my action is $a$" while FDT intervenes on the type). So the P-FDT intervention changes the function everywhere its output matters: the agent's own behavior at *all* observation histories she might have received, and every predictor's model of that behavior. I will adopt this "global type intervention" reading throughout, and flag it as an interpretive commitment beyond the footnote's terse text.

2. **"Same policy" across agents.** In dilemmas with symmetric agents, the relevant dependence is on *being the same kind of reasoner*, not on implementing a literally identical policy. The paper handles this via the graph $G$: arrows run from the decision-function node to the twin's action node because the twin computes the same function. In P-FDT, the corresponding link says the twin's induced policy subjunctively depends on the agent's. Exactly when two decision procedures count as "computing the same function" — up to extensional equality? up to algorithmic similarity? — is not settled in the source; I treat it as an input to the dependence graph, in the same spirit as footnote 4's professed agnosticism about the metaphysics of computations.

### 3.2 Why the extra level matters: two ways it can differ

The footnote says the difference "only matters in multi-agent dilemmas so far as we can tell." Why? Because intervening on the policy rather than the value-at-$x$ changes which counterfactuals the agent can coherently consider. Two consequences stand out.

**(a) Observation counterfactuals become first-class.** The action-level formulation already allows the agent to imagine having made *different observations* — the paper's treatment of the transparent Newcomb problem requires intervening on $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G},\mathrm{full})$ versus $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G},\mathrm{empty})$, separate nodes for the decision function's output at each possible observation. Policy-level intervention unifies these: one node, $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G}) = \pi$, whose variation changes the agent's response at *every* history simultaneously, and thereby changes which histories she is likely to face (because predictors decide what to show her based on how she would respond). This is the essence of the paper's remark, in discussing mechanical blackmail, that "FDT says that agents should entertain the hypothesis that, if their action were different, they might have *made different observations*." P-FDT builds that principle directly into the formalism: choosing a policy is choosing a whole pattern of response, and the *distribution over observations* is itself downstream of that pattern.

**(b) Multi-agent dilemmas get a well-defined unit of coordination.** When several agents' behaviors depend on a shared decision function, the action-level formulation evaluates each agent's choice at one decision point, and the theory must repeatedly re-derive "the twin will match me" at each step. The policy formulation evaluates the whole response profile at once, which matters when agents face different observations: cooperation or defection can hinge on matching *conditional* responses, not just unconditional ones.

Let me illustrate both points.

## 4. P-FDT in the classic dilemmas

**Newcomb's problem.** Here P-FDT reduces to action-FDT, since there is a single decision point. Policies $\pi$ differ only at the one history $x_0$ ("standing before the boxes"). Evaluating $\pi_{\text{one}}$ versus $\pi_{\text{two}}$: under $\mathrm{do}(\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G}) = \pi_{\text{one}})$, the predictor's prediction — which subjunctively depends on the same function — is "one-box" with 99% probability, box B is full with probability 0.99, and expected utility is $\$990{,}000$; under $\pi_{\text{two}}$ it is $\$11{,}000$. The agent executes $\pi_{\text{one}}(x_0) = \text{onebox}$. The calculation is identical to Fiona's in the paper.

**Transparent Newcomb.** Two histories: see-full, see-empty. Policy-level intervention on $\pi$: if $\pi(\text{full}) = \text{onebox}$ and $\pi(\text{empty}) = \text{two-box}$, then with probability 0.99 the predictor predicted one-boxing, the box is full, the agent one-boxes and gets \$1,000,000; with probability 0.01 the prediction erred, the agent sees an empty box, two-boxes, and gets \$1,000. This policy is the argmax, so facing two full boxes the agent leaves the \$1,000 — exactly the paper's conclusion, now derived from a single intervention rather than from selecting among the two nodes $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G},\mathrm{full})$ and $\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G},\mathrm{empty})$. Note the crucial feature: the agent does *not* choose the act that maximizes utility *given her observation*; she chooses the *response profile* whose subjunctive consequences are best, and then applies her observation. This is the formal content of "acting as one would have precommitted to act," with no precommitment machinery needed.

**XOR and mechanical blackmail.** Write the blackmailer's behavior as a function of the agent's policy: the letter is sent iff the blackmailer predicts payment-on-receipt. Under $\mathrm{do}(\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G}) = \pi_{\text{refuse}})$, the blackmailer predicts refusal, the letter is (with overwhelming probability) never sent, and the agent's expected utility is that of an unblackmailed agent. Under $\pi_{\text{pay}}$, she is blackmailed and loses \$1,000 (or, for a fallible blackmailer, an expected amount that is still negative relative to refusal until the error rate is tiny — the paper computes the crossover at a 0.011% error rate). The agent refuses even while holding the letter, because her hypothetical for "my policy is a refusing policy" is a hypothetical in which she was probably never blackmailed. Here the policy formulation earns its keep: the observation "I received a letter" is itself downstream of the policy, so a theory whose unit of choice is the observation-indexed action would face an awkward tension — one the paper resolves, at the action level, by having the agent switch *which node she intervenes on* when observations change. P-FDT makes this automatic: there is one intervention point, and the evidence simply enters the expectation.

**Smoking lesion.** The dependence graph connects the policy node to the agent's action but *not* to cancer: whether the lesion is present does not subjunctively depend on the agent's response function. So the intervention leaves $\Pr(\text{lesion}) = p$ fixed, and smoking dominates by exactly the smoking utility $p \cdot \$1{,}000$. One rule, again, prescribes one-boxing *and* smoking.

## 5. Where the policy level genuinely bites

### 5.1 Symmetric multi-agent dilemmas

In the psychological twin prisoner's dilemma, two agents implement the same decision function. At the action level, each considers "if my function outputs *defect* here, my twin's does too." P-FDT instead evaluates *policies*: if the shared policy is $\pi_{\text{cooperate}}$, both cooperate and each gets \$1,000,000; if $\pi_{\text{defect}}$, each gets \$1,000. Cooperating wins. The advantage of the policy framing appears when the agents' observations differ. Consider a coordination variant: two symmetric agents each observe a private signal $s_i$ (say, a random color shown to each) and must each choose "go" or "wait"; they succeed iff both go. If the signals are independent, the optimal subjunctive question is "which *conditional* response to my signal should we both run?" — a question about the policy $\pi: \{r, b\} \to \{\text{go}, \text{wait}\}$, not about any single act. The action-level formalism of the paper has no node for "my response to the signal I didn't receive"; the policy node does. Similarly, in the paper's voting example — ten agents each deciding whether to pay \$1,000 to vote, each getting \$1,000,000 iff all ten vote — the P-FDT agent recognizes that the other nine face isomorphic decision problems with no relevant differences, and that their policies therefore subjunctively depend on hers; she votes. The policy framing makes precise what "isomorphic with no relevant differences" means: the agents' induced policies coincide on the histories they face.

### 5.2 Repeated and sequential self-coordination

The paper's gym example — an agent who wants to have exercised but dislikes each individual workout — is naturally a policy problem. The decision at Tuesday's gym visit is not causally linked to Thursday's, but the *policy* "skip when the day arrives" is a single object implemented many times. Under $\mathrm{do}(\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G}) = \pi_{\text{attend}})$, the agent attends every session and gains fitness; under $\pi_{\text{skip}}$, she never does. P-FDT selects $\pi_{\text{attend}}$ and therefore shows up on Tuesday. This is also the structure underlying the paper's reflection argument: FDT "endors[es] its own use" and needs no precommitments precisely because the policy-level agent already acts as she would ideally have precommitted — the precommitment target *is* the object of choice.

## 6. Philosophical reading and open questions

**The global perspective.** The paper characterizes FDT as choosing "from a global perspective," asking "What would the entire universe look like if the FDT algorithm behaved differently?" P-FDT literalizes this: the object of choice is a decision function's input–output profile, and the counterfactual varies the profile while respecting logical, mathematical, nomic, and causal structure — everything except those things that subjunctively depend on the profile. On this reading, the theory is not about causally influencing the past or controlling abstract mathematics; it is about choosing, among the responses one's decision procedure could exhibit, the one whose total web of subjunctive consequences is best — the same kind of imaginative act a deterministic CDT agent performs when she contemplates her action "could have" differed, one level up.

**Unresolved points.** Several choices are left open by the source, and honest application of P-FDT requires acknowledging them:

1. **Policy equivalence.** Which physical systems count as implementing "the same policy" is supplied by the dependence graph $G$, but no criterion for constructing $G$ is given. This is the paper's admitted largest open problem, inherited wholesale by P-FDT.
2. **Scope of the policy space.** Should $\Pi$ include randomized policies, or policies sensitive to memory and past observations? The source is silent; the natural reading, consistent with its examples, allows any computable deterministic mapping from full observation histories to actions, with stochasticity treated via explicit "luck" variables as in the paper's models.
3. **Action-level vs. policy-level divergence.** The footnote's claim that the difference "only matters in multi-agent dilemmas so far as we can tell" is itself flagged as tentative ("so far as we can tell"). My own view, developed in §5.2, is that sequential self-coordination problems already exercise the policy level, even with a single agent; and blackmail problems arguably do too, since the observation distribution depends on the policy. Whether these cases produce *different prescriptions* from action-FDT (rather than just cleaner derivations) depends on details the source does not fix — for instance, whether the action-level agent is granted observation-indexed decision nodes, as in the paper's transparent-Newcomb treatment. Where the two formulations can be made to agree, they do; where they cannot, I take the policy formulation to be the more principled, since it makes the feedback from policy to observation distribution explicit rather than handled by node-switching.

**Fairness.** The paper's fairness standard — problems where "the outcome depends only on the agent's behavior in the dilemma at hand" — carries over naturally: Newcomb's predictor punishes *two-boxers*, not rational agents, and P-FDT agents are claimed to match or beat rivals in all fair problems. Whether a formal criterion of fairness exists remains open.

## 7. Conclusion

Policy functional decision theory takes the core insight of functional decision theory — evaluate your decision as the output of a fixed mathematical function, and weigh counterfactuals in which that function's output differs — and applies it at the right grain: to the *policy*, the full mapping from observations to actions. Formally, this replaces the action-level intervention $\mathrm{do}(\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G},\underline{x}) = a)$ with $\mathrm{do}(\mathrm{fdt}(\underline{P},\underline{G}) = \pi)$, followed by executing $\pi(x)$. The payoff is threefold: a unified treatment of dilemmas where observations are themselves downstream of the agent's dispositions (blackmail, transparent Newcomb); a natural unit for coordination among subjunctively dependent agents (twins, coalitions, voting); and a built-in account of why such agents need no precommitments — the precommitment is the very thing being chosen. The theory inherits FDT's central open problem, the construction of the subjunctive-dependence structure $G$, and adds questions of its own about policy equivalence and the exact boundary where policy-level and action-level formulations diverge. But as a normative ideal — choose the response function whose subjunctive consequences are best, then live it out — P-FDT is, in my reading, the cleanest available statement of what functional decision theorists have been reaching for all along.

*Reference: E. Yudkowsky and N. Soares, "Functional Decision Theory: A New Theory of Instrumental Rationality," arXiv:1710.05060 (esp. §5, §9, and footnote 16).*
