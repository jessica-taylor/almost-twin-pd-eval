# Causation, Counterfactuals, and Choice: A Mathematical Foundation for Causal Decision Theory

## 1. Introduction: The Need for Causal Deliberation

Standard Bayesian decision theory instructs an agent to choose an act that maximizes expected utility. Yet the formalization of "expected utility" hinges entirely on how an agent evaluates the relationship between an available act $A$ and a prospective outcome $O$. In the early formalizations of decision theory (such as Richard Jeffrey’s evidential framework), the value of an act was assessed via conditional probability:

$$V(A) = \sum_{j} P(O_j \mid A) \, u(O_j)$$

This equation formalizes **Evidential Decision Theory (EDT)**. Under EDT, an act is evaluated according to how welcome the news would be that the act was taken. If observing that you chose $A$ provides strong statistical evidence that an advantageous state of the world holds, EDT assigns $A$ a high desirability.

However, standard statistical conditioning conflates *correlation* with *causation*. When an act and an outcome share an unobserved common cause, taking the act can serve as excellent evidence for a desirable outcome without doing anything to bring that outcome about. In such settings, EDT frequently leads to what critics describe as an irrational policy: acting to manipulate symptoms rather than underlying causes.

**Causal Decision Theory (CDT)** arose as a mathematically rigorous response to this defect. CDT posits that rational deliberation must isolate what an act *physically or counterfactually brings about* from what it merely *indicates*. Rather than maximizing news value, a CDT agent maximizes **causal expected utility**: the expected payoff generated by the causal descendants of the choice, holding fixed everything outside the agent’s causal influence.

This essay provides a comprehensive formal account of CDT. We examine its mathematical formulations—ranging from the counterfactual conditional approach of Allan Gibbard and William Harper to David Lewis’s imaging technique—contrast its prescriptions with EDT across canonical problems like Newcomb's Paradox and Solomon's Problem, examine modern structural causal modeling (Pearl's $do$-calculus) as an operational engine, and analyze the acute theoretical challenges that CDT faces in unstable and adversarial environments.

## 2. Formal Frameworks of Causal Decision Theory

To formulate CDT precisely, we consider an agent facing a finite set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive actions $\mathcal{A} = \{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_m\}$ and a set of possible outcomes $\mathcal{O} = \{O_1, O_2, \dots, O_n\}$. Let $u: \mathcal{O} \to \mathbb{R}$ denote a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function reflecting the agent’s subjective evaluation of each outcome. The agent maintains a subjective probability distribution $P$ over an algebra of propositions defined on a space of possible worlds $\Omega$.

The central formal challenge of CDT is to define an action-indexed probability distribution over outcomes, denoted $P_A(O_j)$, that represents the probability that outcome $O_j$ *would result if the agent were to perform act $A$*, distinct from the conditional credence $P(O_j \mid A)$.

### 2.1 The Gibbard–Harper Formulation: Counterfactual Conditionals

Allan Gibbard and William Harper (1981) formulated CDT by extending the probability space to include counterfactual conditionals of the form $A \to O_j$, interpreted as "if $A$ were done, $O_j$ would obtain." The causal expected utility $U(A)$ is defined as:

$$U(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} P(A \to O_j) \, u(O_j) \tag{1}$$

Here, $P(A \to O_j)$ is the agent's prior subjective probability assigned to the counterfactual proposition $A \to O_j$.

The divide between CDT and EDT reduces to whether $P(A \to O_j) = P(O_j \mid A)$. If this identity held universally, CDT and EDT would be mathematically indistinguishable. However, David Lewis (1976) proved a foundational "triviality theorem": there is no non-trivial proposition $A \to B$ whose unconditional probability equals the conditional probability $P(B \mid A)$ for all probability distributions closed under conditionalization. Specifically, Lewis demonstrated that any probability measure satisfying $P(A \to B) = P(B \mid A)$ across arbitrary propositions can assign non-zero probability to at most four distinct mutually exclusive propositions.

Gibbard and Harper established that $P(A \to O_j) = P(O_j \mid A)$ holds if and only if the act $A$ is statistically independent of the counterfactual conditional $A \to O_j$:

$$P\big((A \to O_j) \mid A\big) = P(A \to O_j)$$

Under the standard Stalnaker–Lewis semantics for conditionals, this independence fails precisely in scenarios where the performance of act $A$ is correlated with antecedent conditions that govern the truth of the conditional itself. When an agent's choice correlates with a prior state of nature that the choice cannot causally affect, evidential conditionalization updates the probability of that background state, whereas counterfactual evaluation leaves it unaltered.

### 2.2 Lewis’s Formulation: Imaging on Possible Worlds

A perennial mathematical objection to Equation (1) is that it requires an explicit probability measure over counterfactual propositions, whose semantic status and formal algebra can be philosophically contentious. To operationalize counterfactual evaluation directly on standard possible worlds, David Lewis (1981) introduced the method of **imaging**.

Let $\Omega$ be a set of possible worlds, each fully specifying the physical state of affairs, history, and causal laws. The agent possesses a prior probability measure $P$ on $\Omega$. We define a distance or similarity metric $d: \Omega \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ such that for every world $w \in \Omega$ and any non-empty proposition $A \subseteq \Omega$, there exists a unique world $w_A \in A$ that is minimally different from $w$ while making $A$ true.

Under Lewisian imaging, if the agent were to enact $A$, the probability mass $P(w)$ assigned to world $w$ does not conditionalize (which would discard all worlds where $\neg A$ holds and rescale the remainder by $1/P(A)$). Instead, the mass $P(w)$ is physically transposed to its closest $A$-counterpart:

$$P_A(w') = \sum_{\{w \in \Omega : w_A = w'\}} P(w) \tag{2}$$

The causal expected utility of an action $A$ is then simply the expected utility under the imaged probability measure $P_A$:

$$U(A) = \sum_{w' \in \Omega} P_A(w') \, u(w') \tag{3}$$

Notice that if $w \in A$, then $w_A = w$, meaning that worlds already satisfying $A$ keep their assigned mass. If an act $A$ cannot causally affect a background condition $K$, then for every world $w$, the world $w_A$ preserves the truth value of $K$. Consequently, $P_A(K) = P(K)$, ensuring that an agent cannot alter the likelihood of past or causally isolated events by choosing $A$.

### 2.3 Dependency Hypotheses and the State-Partition Formulation

A mathematically transparent formulation that bridges Gibbard–Harper conditionals and Lewisian imaging is the **dependency hypothesis** framework. 

Let $\mathcal{K} = \{K_1, K_2, \dots, K_r\}$ be a mutually exclusive and exhaustive partition of possible states of affairs. Each $K \in \mathcal{K}$ represents a maximally specific hypothesis concerning the causal laws and background conditions that are completely outside the agent’s causal influence. A dependency hypothesis $K$ completely determines which outcome $O_j$ will result for each action $A \in \mathcal{A}$. That is, under $K_i$, the conditional $A \to O(A, K_i)$ is determined with certainty.

Because the state $K_i$ is causally independent of the agent’s current choice of act $A$, the counterfactual probability that $K_i$ would obtain if $A$ were enacted is simply the prior credence $P(K_i)$. The causal expected utility simplifies to:

$$U(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(K_i) \, u\big(O(A, K_i)\big) \tag{4}$$

By contrast, the evidential expected utility $V(A)$ conditions the partition on $A$:

$$V(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(K_i \mid A) \, u\big(O(A, K_i)\big) \tag{5}$$

Equations (4) and (5) represent the mathematical core of the debate. EDT uses $P(K_i \mid A)$, allowing the choice of $A$ to alter the weights of the background states via Bayesian conditioning. CDT uses the unconditional credence $P(K_i)$, holding fixed the distribution over background causal structures regardless of what action is evaluated.

## 3. An Operational Modern Interpretation: Structural Causal Models

The source reference correctly notes that CDT has historically been accused of formal vagueness because early formulations rested on abstract counterfactual metrics or primitive conditionals. However, in contemporary applied mathematics and artificial intelligence, an operational interpretation exists: Judea Pearl’s **Structural Causal Models (SCMs)** and the **$do$-calculus**. 

While not explicitly unified in early philosophical articles, framing CDT through SCMs resolves ambiguities regarding how counterfactual distributions are computed in concrete decision scenarios.

### 3.1 Structural Causal Models and Interventions

An SCM is a tuple $M = \langle U, V, \mathcal{F}, P(U) \rangle$, where:
- $U = \{U_1, \dots, U_k\}$ is a set of exogenous (unobserved background) variables governed by a joint distribution $P(U)$.
- $V = \{V_1, \dots, V_m\}$ is a set of endogenous (observable) variables.
- $\mathcal{F} = \{f_1, \dots, f_m\}$ is a set of deterministic structural equations such that each $V_i = f_i(\text{Pa}_i, U_i)$, where $\text{Pa}_i \subseteq V \setminus \{V_i\}$ denotes the direct causal parents of $V_i$.

A decision problem is modeled by identifying the action as a choice variable $A \in V$ and the payoff as a utility variable $Y \in V$. 

The core operational tool representing a causal choice is Pearl’s **intervention operator**, denoted $\text{do}(A = a)$. An intervention does not condition on $A = a$; rather, it replaces the structural equation $A = f_A(\text{Pa}_A, U_A)$ with the constant assignment $A = a$, severing all incoming edges from $\text{Pa}_A$ into $A$ in the underlying directed acyclic graph (DAG). 

Using the truncated factorization product, the post-intervention distribution is given by:

$$P(V \setminus \{A\} \mid \text{do}(A = a)) = \prod_{V_i \neq A} P(V_i \mid \text{Pa}_i)$$

Consequently, the operational CDT decision rule for an agent evaluating action $a \in \mathcal{A}$ is:

$$\mathcal{A}_{\text{CDT}} = \arg\max_{a} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \text{do}(A = a)] = \arg\max_{a} \sum_{y} y \, P(Y = y \mid \text{do}(A = a)) \tag{6}$$

In contrast, the EDT decision rule maximizes observational conditional expectation:

$$\mathcal{A}_{\text{EDT}} = \arg\max_{a} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A = a] = \arg\max_{a} \sum_{y} y \, P(Y = y \mid A = a) \tag{7}$$

Equation (6) mathematically clarifies CDT: it holds fixed all exogenous background distributions $P(U)$ and non-descendant causes of $A$, evaluating choices purely by forward propagation through the mutilated causal graph.

```
       Common Confounder / Past State (U)
                 /             \
                /               \
               v                 v
          Action (A) -------> Outcome (Y)
          
     EDT evaluates:  P(Y | A)      [Propagates info along both A -> Y and A <- U -> Y]
     CDT evaluates:  P(Y | do(A))  [Cuts the arrow U -> A, propagating only along A -> Y]
```

## 4. Canonical Demonstrations and Case Studies

To see how CDT evaluates alternative policies compared to EDT, we examine classic decision problems where evidential correlations diverge from causal dependencies.

### 4.1 Solomon’s Problem: King David and Bathsheba

Gibbard and Harper introduced the historical-psychological problem of King David to illustrate why EDT appears to prescribe magical thinking.

#### The Setup
- **Action space:** David can send for Bathsheba ($A_1$) or abstain ($A_0$).
- **Background partition:** Kings are either genetically charismatic ($K_c$) or uncharismatic ($K_u$). A king's charisma is determined in early childhood and cannot be changed ($A$ cannot cause $K$).
- **Causal structure:** Charismatic kings have an imposing, noble bearing that deters revolt; uncharismatic kings have an ignoble bearing that invites revolt. Sending for another man's wife is an unjust act ($A_1$). Unjust acts *do not cause revolts*; only a lack of charisma causes revolts.
- **Statistical correlation:** Charismatic kings almost always refrain from unjust acts ($P(A_1 \mid K_c) = 0.05$), while uncharismatic kings frequently commit unjust acts ($P(A_1 \mid K_u) = 0.80$).
- **Payoffs:** David desires Bathsheba (utility $+10$), but intensely fears a revolt (utility $-100$). A peaceful reign has utility $0$. Let utilities be:
  - $u(A_1 \wedge \text{no revolt}) = 10$
  - $u(A_1 \wedge \text{revolt}) = -90$
  - $u(A_0 \wedge \text{no revolt}) = 0$
  - $u(A_0 \wedge \text{revolt}) = -100$

Let the prior probability of being charismatic be $P(K_c) = 0.5$, so $P(K_u) = 0.5$. Let the probability of revolt given charisma be $P(R \mid K_c) = 0.1$, and given lack of charisma be $P(R \mid K_u) = 0.9$. Crucially, because $A$ has no causal effect on revolt:

$$P(R \mid \text{do}(A), K) = P(R \mid K)$$

#### The EDT Evaluation
An evidentialist calculates $V(A_1)$ vs $V(A_0)$. First, using Bayes' rule:

$$P(A_1) = P(A_1 \mid K_c)P(K_c) + P(A_1 \mid K_u)P(K_u) = (0.05)(0.5) + (0.80)(0.5) = 0.025 + 0.40 = 0.425$$

The posterior probability that David is charismatic given that he takes Bathsheba is:

$$P(K_c \mid A_1) = \frac{P(A_1 \mid K_c)P(K_c)}{P(A_1)} = \frac{0.025}{0.425} \approx 0.0588$$

Thus, $P(K_u \mid A_1) \approx 0.9412$. The evidential probability of facing a revolt if he summons her is:

$$P(R \mid A_1) \approx (0.1)(0.0588) + (0.9)(0.9412) = 0.0059 + 0.8471 = 0.853$$

The evidential utility of summoning Bathsheba is:

$$V(A_1) = P(R \mid A_1)(-90) + (1 - P(R \mid A_1))(10) = (0.853)(-90) + (0.147)(10) = -76.77 + 1.47 = -75.3$$

Now evaluate abstaining ($A_0$):

$$P(A_0) = 1 - 0.425 = 0.575$$
$$P(K_c \mid A_0) = \frac{P(A_0 \mid K_c)P(K_c)}{P(A_0)} = \frac{(0.95)(0.5)}{0.575} \approx 0.826$$

Thus, $P(R \mid A_0) \approx (0.1)(0.826) + (0.9)(0.174) = 0.0826 + 0.1566 = 0.2392$.

$$V(A_0) = (0.2392)(-100) + (0.7608)(0) = -23.92$$

Because $V(A_0) > V(A_1)$ ($-23.92 > -75.3$), **EDT recommends abstaining**. EDT treats summoning Bathsheba as terrible news regarding David's innate charisma.

#### The CDT Evaluation
CDT evaluates actions holding fixed the background partition $\mathcal{K} = \{K_c, K_u\}$ with their prior weights $P(K_c) = 0.5$ and $P(K_u) = 0.5$:

$$U(A) = P(K_c) \, U(A \mid K_c) + P(K_u) \, U(A \mid K_u)$$

Conditional on David being charismatic ($K_c$):
- $U(A_1 \mid K_c) = (0.1)(-90) + (0.9)(10) = -9 + 9 = 0$
- $U(A_0 \mid K_c) = (0.1)(-100) + (0.9)(0) = -10$
- Dominance difference: $U(A_1 \mid K_c) - U(A_0 \mid K_c) = 0 - (-10) = +10$

Conditional on David being uncharismatic ($K_u$):
- $U(A_1 \mid K_u) = (0.9)(-90) + (0.1)(10) = -81 + 1 = -80$
- $U(A_0 \mid K_u) = (0.9)(-100) + (0.1)(0) = -90$
- Dominance difference: $U(A_1 \mid K_u) - U(A_0 \mid K_u) = -80 - (-90) = +10$

Aggregating across the partition:

$$U(A_1) = 0.5(0) + 0.5(-80) = -40$$
$$U(A_0) = 0.5(-10) + 0.5(-90) = -50$$

Because $U(A_1) > U(A_0)$ ($-40 > -50$), **CDT recommends summoning Bathsheba**. 

The causal rationale is transparent: David's decision does not alter his genetic make-up. If he is charismatic, summoning Bathsheba yields $0$ instead of $-10$. If he is uncharismatic, summoning her yields $-80$ instead of $-90$. In both counterfactual branches, summoning Bathsheba strictly dominates abstaining by exactly $10$ units of utility (the pleasure of her company). CDT respects this strict state-by-state dominance, whereas EDT violates dominance because the choice serves as an epistemic diagnostic of a pre-existing state.

### 4.2 Newcomb’s Paradox

Newcomb’s Paradox is the canonical arena where CDT and EDT clash over the concept of dominance versus high-probability reward.

#### Formal Parameters
- **Boxes:** An opaque box $B$ and a transparent box $A$ containing $\$1,000$.
- **Actions:** One-box ($A_{\text{one}}$, take only $B$) or Two-box ($A_{\text{two}}$, take $A$ and $B$).
- **Predictor:** An exceptionally accurate predictor who observed the agent yesterday and placed $\$1,000,000$ in box $B$ if and only if she predicted $A_{\text{one}}$.
- Let $M$ denote the presence of $\$1,000,000$ in box $B$, and $E$ denote an empty box $B$. The dependency hypothesis partition is $\mathcal{K} = \{K_M, K_E\}$, where:
  - $K_M$: Box $B$ contains $\$1,000,000$.
  - $K_E$: Box $B$ contains $\$0$.
- Assume linear utility for money: $u(\$x) = x$.
- Let the predictor's reliability be $p = 0.99$:
  - $P(K_M \mid A_{\text{one}}) = 0.99$, $P(K_E \mid A_{\text{one}}) = 0.01$
  - $P(K_M \mid A_{\text{two}}) = 0.01$, $P(K_E \mid A_{\text{two}}) = 0.99$

#### The EDT Analysis
$$V(A_{\text{one}}) = (0.99)(1,000,000) + (0.01)(0) = 990,000$$
$$V(A_{\text{two}}) = (0.01)(1,001,000) + (0.99)(1,000) = 10,010 + 990 = 11,000$$

Since $V(A_{\text{one}}) \gg V(A_{\text{two}})$, EDT unambiguously prescribes taking **only Box B** ("one-boxing").

#### The CDT Analysis
CDT fixes the partition $\mathcal{K} = \{K_M, K_E\}$ with whatever prior belief $P(K_M) = q$ the agent holds. Crucially, the physical contents of Box $B$ were determined yesterday. The causal graph has no path from the agent’s current action $A$ to the contents of Box $B$:

$$\mathbb{E}[B \mid \text{do}(A_{\text{one}})] = \mathbb{E}[B \mid \text{do}(A_{\text{two}})]$$

Evaluating the utilities:

$$U(A_{\text{one}}) = q(1,000,000) + (1-q)(0) = 1,000,000\,q$$
$$U(A_{\text{two}}) = q(1,001,000) + (1-q)(1,000) = 1,000,000\,q + 1,000$$

For any prior credence $q \in [0, 1]$:

$$U(A_{\text{two}}) - U(A_{\text{one}}) = 1,000 > 0$$

Two-boxing strictly causally dominates one-boxing. Whatever is in Box $B$ is already there; taking Box $A$ secures an unconditional extra $\$1,000$. Therefore, **CDT commands two-boxing**.

The tension between CDT and EDT is starkest here: EDT agents emerge from the experiment with $\$1,000,000$ on average, whereas CDT agents walk away with $\$1,000$. The causalist defends this outcome by arguing that the $\$1,000,000$ was lost yesterday during prediction, not today during choice: an agent cannot causally reach back in time to alter yesterday's past placement.

## 5. Theoretical Challenges and Instabilities

While CDT elegantly resolves common-cause dilemmas like King David's problem, it confronts severe structural challenges when applied to environments involving self-locating instability, dynamic interaction, and adversarial predictors.

### 5.1 Deliberational Instability: The Psychopath Button and Death in Damascus

A profound limitation of standard CDT arises in cases where an action provides evidence about a state of nature that inverts the act's own causal desirability, creating an endless cycle of self-defeating preferences. Andy Egan (2007) formalized this with several counterexamples.

#### The Psychopath Button
- **Setting:** Paul can press a button ($A_1$) to eliminate all psychopaths from the world, or refrain ($A_0$). Paul strongly values eliminating psychopaths (utility $+100$). However, Paul assigns utility $-1,000$ to dying.
- **Correlation:** Paul knows that 99% of people who would press this button are themselves psychopaths ($P(\text{Psy} \mid A_1) = 0.99$). If Paul is a psychopath, pressing the button kills him.
- **Causal structure:** Pressing the button does not *make* Paul a psychopath; it merely executes all existing psychopaths.

If Paul currently believes he is an ordinary non-psychopath with prior $P(\text{Psy}) = 0.01$:
- Counterfactually, holding fixed $P(\text{Psy}) = 0.01$, the causal expected utility of pressing the button is overwhelmingly positive:
  $$U(A_1) \approx 0.99(+100) + 0.01(-900) = 99 - 9 = +90 > U(A_0) = 0$$
- Thus, CDT initially prescribes that Paul **should press the button**.

However, as soon as Paul forms the intention or evaluates the counterfactual under the impending choice $A_1$, he acquires evidence that he is a psychopath. If he is convinced that choosing $A_1$ implies he is a psychopath ($P(\text{Psy} \mid A_1) = 0.99$), pressing will almost certainly result in his own death ($U \approx -900$). Under that belief, pressing is catastrophic, and he must reverse his decision to $A_0$. But as soon as he chooses $A_0$, the evidence that he is a psychopath vanishes, making $A_1$ attractive once more.

#### Death in Damascus
A similar failure mode occurs in the classic *Death in Damascus* puzzle:
- Death predicts whether you will be in Damascus ($D$) or Aleppo ($A$) tomorrow and meets you there.
- If you go to Damascus, Death is almost certainly waiting in Damascus. If you switch to Aleppo, Death predicted the switch and waits in Aleppo.
- For a CDT agent at any given moment:
  - If you plan to go to Damascus, your credence that Death is in Damascus approaches $1$. Holding that location fixed, your causal expectation for going to Aleppo is superior (you avoid Death).
  - But as soon as you choose Aleppo, your rational credence shifts to Death being in Aleppo, making Damascus causally superior.

In these problems, CDT lacks a pure-strategy fixed point. Allan Gibbard and William Harper, along with later work by James Joyce (2012), addressed this via the concept of **ratifiability**.

An action $A$ is **ratifiable** if and only if $A$ maximizes causal expected utility relative to the probability distribution updated on the assumption that $A$ will be performed:

$$U(A \mid A) \ge U(A' \mid A) \quad \forall A' \in \mathcal{A} \tag{8}$$

In Death in Damascus and the Psychopath Button, *no pure action is ratifiable*. Joyce and other defenders of CDT propose that in non-ratifiable dilemmas, rational deliberation requires the agent to randomize, converging to a mixed strategy (a Deliberational Equilibrium) where the causal expected utilities of the options balance out.

### 5.2 Adversarial Exploitation and Diachronic Dutch Books

Recent mathematical critiques by Caspar Oesterheld, Vincent Conitzer (2021), and Jack Spencer (2020) have revealed an even more severe systemic vulnerability: CDT agents can be systematically exploited by informed adversaries, entering into guaranteed losses or **diachronic Dutch books**.

#### The Adversarial Offer
Consider the following game formalized by Oesterheld and Conitzer:
- A seller presents two opaque boxes ($B_1$ and $B_2$) to a buyer.
- The buyer may purchase at most one box for $\$1$, or purchase neither.
- Yesterday, the seller predicted the buyer's choice. In each box that the seller predicted the buyer would **not** acquire, the seller placed $\$3$.
- The seller's prediction is accurate with probability $p = 0.75$.

Let us analyze the buyer's reasoning under CDT:
- The buyer evaluates the causal expected value of buying $B_1$:
  - The money is already in the boxes. The seller placed $\$3$ in $B_1$ if she predicted the buyer would choose $B_2$ or neither.
  - The seller predicted the buyer would not acquire $B_1$ with high probability if the buyer selects an alternative or randomizes.
  - More fundamentally, because the seller can predict at most one target, the seller placed $\$3$ in *at least one box* (and possibly both, if she predicted "neither").
  - Therefore, the total money distributed across the two boxes is at least $\$3$. By symmetry, the average unconditioned causal expectation of the content of a box is:
    $$\mathbb{E}[\text{Content}(B_i)] \ge \frac{\$3}{2} = \$1.50$$
  - Since $\$1.50 > \$1.00$ (the cost of the box), CDT computes a positive net causal expected value:
    $$U(\text{Buy } B_1) = \$1.50 - \$1.00 = +\$0.50 > U(\text{Buy neither}) = \$0$$
- CDT commands the agent to purchase a box (say, $B_1$).

However, the seller predicted that the buyer would purchase $B_1$ with probability $0.75$. Therefore, the probability that $B_1$ actually contains $\$3$ is only $1 - 0.75 = 0.25$. 
The actual realized expected payout to the CDT agent is:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{Realized Profit}] = (0.25)(\$3) - \$1 = \$0.75 - \$1.00 = -\$0.25$$

The seller reliably extracts $\$0.25$ from the CDT buyer on every iteration. 

Because CDT agents hold fixed the prior distribution over the predictor's past actions when calculating causal returns, they view the contents of the boxes as an invariant lottery with expectation $\$1.50$, ignoring the fact that their systematic decision rule guarantees they are walking into a trap set by an accurate predictor.

## 6. Synthesis and Comparative Summary

The essential distinctions between Evidential Decision Theory and Causal Decision Theory are summarized in Table 1 below:

| Feature | Evidential Decision Theory (EDT) | Causal Decision Theory (CDT) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Guiding Maxim** | Choose the act that provides the best news. | Choose the act that causes the best outcome. |
| **Core Mathematical Formula** | $V(A) = \sum_j P(O_j \mid A) \, u(O_j)$ | $U(A) = \sum_j P(A \to O_j) \, u(O_j)$ |
| **Probability Shift Mechanism** | Standard Bayesian conditioning: $P(\cdot \mid A)$ | Lewis Imaging $P_A(\cdot)$ or Pearl's Intervention $P(\cdot \mid \text{do}(A))$ |
| **State Partition Weights** | Posterior credences $P(K_i \mid A)$ | Fixed prior credences $P(K_i)$ |
| **Treatment of Common Causes** | Confounds correlation with agency; manages symptoms | Isolates interventions; respects state dominance |
| **Verdict on Newcomb's Paradox** | **One-box** (secures $\$1,000,000$ in expectation) | **Two-box** (secures dominant extra $\$1,000$) |
| **Verdict on King David's Problem** | **Abstain** (avoids bad news of low charisma) | **Summon Bathsheba** (dominates; charisma is invariant) |
| **Primary Pathologies** | Susceptible to superstitious/diagnostic avoidance | Deliberational instability, adversarial exploitation |

## 7. Operational Guidelines for Applying CDT to Unfamiliar Cases

To enable a practitioner, statistician, or philosopher to evaluate arbitrary, unfamiliar decision problems using Causal Decision Theory, the theoretical machinery can be synthesized into an operational four-step procedure:

1. **Step 1: Construct the Full Exogenous Partition (Dependency Hypotheses)**
   Identify all propositions $\mathcal{K} = \{K_1, \dots, K_r\}$ that:
   - Settle the deterministic mapping from each prospective act $A \in \mathcal{A}$ to an outcome $O \in \mathcal{O}$.
   - Are causally upstream of, or causally isolated from, the action $A$. That is, verify that for all $i$ and all $A$, the counterfactual truth value of $K_i$ is invariant under the intervention $\text{do}(A)$.

2. **Step 2: Assign Prior Credences to the Partition**
   Establish the unconditional subjective probabilities $P(K_i)$. **Do not** condition these probabilities on the candidate action $A$. The central axiom of CDT requires that the agent evaluate acts using the distribution over dependency hypotheses held *prior* to assuming the act has occurred:
   $$P_A(K_i) = P(K_i)$$

3. **Step 3: Evaluate Causal Expected Utility**
   For each available candidate action $A \in \mathcal{A}$, compute:
   $$U(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(K_i) \, u\big(O(A, K_i)\big)$$
   Equivalently, if working with an explicit Structural Causal Model, set $A = a$ via the intervention $\text{do}(A = a)$, sever all incoming causal edges to $A$, and evaluate $\mathbb{E}[u(Y) \mid \text{do}(A = a)]$.

4. **Step 4: Verify Ratifiability (Stability Check)**
   Check whether the optimal action $A^* = \arg\max_A U(A)$ is ratifiable. Compute the updated credence $P(\cdot \mid A^*)$. If $A^*$ ceases to maximize causal expected utility relative to $P(\cdot \mid A^*)$, the problem exhibits deliberational instability. In such cases, standard pure-strategy CDT fails to provide a unique stable prescription, and the agent must resolve the problem by identifying a mixed-strategy equilibrium over acts.

---

## 8. Conclusion

Causal Decision Theory provides a mathematically rigorous formulation of instrumental rationality. By substituting counterfactual probabilities (or surgical interventions) for standard conditional credences, CDT frees rational agents from the "magical thinking" that plagues Evidential Decision Theory in common-cause scenarios like King David's dilemma. It formalizes the fundamental intuition that deliberating agents should treat their choices as active physical interventions that manipulate the future, rather than as passive statistical observations that diagnose the past.

Yet this insistence on holding background dependency hypotheses fixed is both CDT's defining strength and its greatest theoretical vulnerability. In asymmetric, highly correlated environments—such as Newcomb's Paradox, adversarial bargaining, and games against accurate predictive models—CDT agents consistently earn less money than evidentialists or agents employing alternative coordination logics (such as Functional Decision Theory). 

Whether this performance gap represents an intrinsic failure of CDT or merely highlights the tragic predicament of rational actors operating in hostile, non-causal environments remains one of the central foundational debates of modern decision sciences.
