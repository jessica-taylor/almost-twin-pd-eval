# P-FDT: Choosing a Policy as the Output of a Decision Function

Policy functional decision theory, or **P-FDT**, starts from a distinction between changing an action and changing the rule that produces it. When an agent deliberates, its immediate physical action is not necessarily the only relevant consequence of its decision procedure. A predictor may have modeled that procedure; another agent may run a copy of it; or the procedure may be called again under a different observation. P-FDT asks how outcomes would differ if the *policy selected by the decision function* were different, while preserving the relevant dependencies between that policy and the rest of the world.

The primary source for this policy formulation is footnote 16 of Yudkowsky and Soares’ *Functional Decision Theory*. It says that the authors’ preferred formulation intervenes on the decision-function node “to choose not an action but a *policy* which maps inputs to actions,” and then applies the agent’s inputs to the selected policy. Most of the paper instead develops an action-output formalization for simplicity.

That distinction matters for interpretation. The paper gives detailed examples and a graphical framework, but it does not supply a complete mathematical specification of policy selection. The formal construction below is therefore an operational proposal: it extends the footnote using the paper’s account of subjunctive dependence, while marking choices the source leaves open.

## 1. Why select policies?

Suppose an agent will later observe either \(x_1\) or \(x_2\), and can choose either \(a\) or \(b\). A deterministic policy is a function

\[
\pi:\{x_1,x_2\}\longrightarrow\{a,b\}.
\]

There are four such policies: always \(a\), always \(b\), and the two conditional policies. Selecting only an action for the currently observed \(x_1\) leaves open what happens at \(x_2\). Selecting a policy specifies both.

This is useful even when only one observation will actually occur. An earlier predictor may have inspected what the agent would do *if* it saw \(x_2\). Similarly, another implementation of the same selected policy might receive \(x_2\), while the present agent receives \(x_1\). The selected policy can therefore matter through entries other than the one applied here and now.

P-FDT does not merely recommend writing a plan and then treating it as a binding contract. Its proposal is about the counterfactuals used to decide: compare possible outputs of the policy-selection computation, propagate their consequences through relevant dependencies, choose the best policy, and apply it to the actual input.

This contrasts with two familiar approaches:

- **Evidential decision theory** (EDT) evaluates an action by conditionalizing on taking it: roughly, \(\mathbb E[U\mid X=x,A=a]\). It asks what taking the action would be evidence of.
- **Causal decision theory** (CDT) evaluates an action using a causal intervention, roughly \(\mathbb E[U\mid X=x,\operatorname{do}(A=a)]\). It asks what physically changing the action would cause.

P-FDT also maximizes expected utility, but uses a different hypothetical: what follows if the relevant decision computation outputs a particular policy? A causal consequence of applying the policy counts, but so can a predictor’s earlier response to that policy.

## 2. A working formalization

Let \(\mathcal X\) be the set of policy inputs and \(\mathcal A(x)\) the actions available at input \(x\). Let

\[
\Pi=\{\pi:\mathcal X\to\mathcal A\mid \pi(x)\in\mathcal A(x)\}
\]

be a specified class of feasible deterministic policies. One can enlarge \(\Pi\) to include randomized policies, but then the model must say who can predict or share the randomization.

Let \(D\) denote the P-FDT policy-selection computation for a specified decision problem, and let \(S\) be a node in a world-model \(M\) representing its selected policy. Actual actions are generated by

\[
A=\operatorname{Apply}(S,X)=S(X).
\]

The model also contains an outcome \(O\), utility \(U(O)\), and whatever variables describe predictors, other agents, physical consequences, and uncertainty.

For each candidate \(\pi\in\Pi\), construct a hypothetical distribution

\[
Q_\pi(\,\cdot\,)
=
P_M\bigl(\,\cdot\,\mid \operatorname{do}_{\!F}(S=\pi)\bigr).
\]

Here \(\operatorname{do}_{\!F}\) is a **functional intervention**: it sets the represented output of \(D\) to \(\pi\), then propagates changes through the dependencies that the model treats as relevant. The subscript is a reminder that this is not automatically an ordinary physical intervention.

An operational P-FDT rule is then

\[
\pi^\star\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}
\mathbb E_{Q_\pi}[U(O)],
\qquad
a^\star=\pi^\star(x_{\mathrm{actual}}).
\]

A tie-breaking convention is needed if several policies maximize value. If no maximum exists, a practical version needs an approximation rule.

This equation should be read with three qualifications.

First, it intervenes on the *output of the policy selector*, not simply on a physical actuator, and not necessarily on the agent’s source code. A predictor of that output may therefore change in the hypothetical.

Second, the model must specify what is held fixed. Exogenous noise and unrelated physical conditions ordinarily remain fixed in distribution; variables downstream through causal or appropriate computational dependencies are recomputed. Merely being correlated with the actual action is not enough.

Third, \(Q_\pi\) is a tool for evaluating a candidate, not necessarily the agent’s posterior belief about what actually occurred. The agent can believe it saw a full box while considering a policy hypothetical in which it would instead have seen an empty one.

The paper’s action-output equations use a graph containing logical as well as causal dependencies. The policy version above borrows that idea, but the footnote itself does not uniquely determine the graph, the policy class, or the treatment of observations. Those are substantive modeling decisions.

## 3. Subjunctive dependence, not correlation alone

The source calls the broad relation relevant to these hypotheticals **subjunctive dependence**. Two physical systems implementing the same computation can depend on its output even if neither physically causes the other. Two correctly functioning calculators evaluating the same sum are a simple analogy. If a twin implements the same decision computation on the same input, the twin’s decision should change with that computation’s output in the hypothetical.

A predictor need not literally contain a perfect copy. Its prediction may depend on a model of the policy, with some probability of error. The strength of that dependence is a matter for the world-model.

Contrast an imaginary smoking-lesion problem. A lesion causes both a desire to smoke and a fatal disease; smoking itself does not cause the disease. If the agent values smoking and changing the selected policy does not change the lesion or disease, then

\[
V(\text{smoke})-V(\text{refrain})=1{,}000.
\]

P-FDT smokes in that model. It does not treat the disease as changing merely because smoking is statistically associated with disease. The source notes complications in formalizing this example—especially uncertainty about preferences and the consistency of the agent’s beliefs—so the conclusion depends on the specified model.

This is the central discipline of P-FDT counterfactuals: **preserve modeled dependencies, not all correlations**. It is also the central difficulty. A graph that says what changes when a logical computation has a different output is not supplied by ordinary causal discovery alone. The source explicitly identifies constructing such a theory of dependencies as an open problem.

There is an additional philosophical complication. If a deterministic computation on fixed inputs in fact outputs \(\pi^\star\), then “that computation outputs a different policy” is logically false, not just physically unlikely. P-FDT needs informative *counterpossible* reasoning. The graphical intervention is a proposed way to organize such reasoning, not a proof that a general account of counterpossibles has been solved.

## 4. P-FDT in Newcomb’s problem

A predictor fills an opaque box with \$1,000,000 if it predicts that the agent will take only that box. A transparent box contains \$1,000. Assume the predictor is correct with probability \(0.99\), under each relevant policy hypothetical, and utility is linear in money.

For a one-observation version, compare constant policies \(\pi_1\) and \(\pi_2\), prescribing one-boxing and two-boxing:

\[
\begin{aligned}
V(\pi_1)&=0.99(1{,}000{,}000)=990{,}000,\\
V(\pi_2)&=0.01(1{,}000{,}000)+1{,}000=11{,}000.
\end{aligned}
\]

P-FDT selects \(\pi_1\). It does not claim that reaching for one box sends a signal into the past. Rather, in the modeled counterfactual where the policy selector outputs a two-boxing policy, the predictor’s model is correspondingly likely to predict two-boxing.

The assumed accuracy is important. An observed record of 99% accuracy across some population does not by itself establish 99% accuracy under every policy intervention. A predictor might be accurate for constant policies but fail on unfamiliar randomized or conditional ones. To apply P-FDT, the response of the predictor to the policies under consideration must be modeled, not inferred from a bare correlation.

### Transparent boxes

Now suppose the agent sees whether the opaque box is full. The predictor fills it if it predicts that the agent would leave the \$1,000 behind **upon seeing it full**. A policy specifies actions at both inputs:

\[
\pi(\text{full}),\qquad \pi(\text{empty}).
\]

Assume a perfect predictor. If \(\pi(\text{full})=\text{one-box}\), the box is full and the payoff is \$1,000,000. If \(\pi(\text{full})=\text{two-box}\), the box is empty and, at best, the payoff is \$1,000. Thus a maximizing policy one-boxes at a full box and two-boxes at an empty box.

This is not the same as saying that a person who has seen the full box can physically alter its past contents. It says that the *policy* prescribing two-boxing on that observation would be associated with a different prediction and generally a different observation.

The paper’s action-output formulation reaches a similar conclusion by changing the decision-function node associated with the observed input. The policy formulation makes the conditional prescription explicit. It also reveals an unresolved question: which evidence should be fixed when comparing policies? An agent must use evidence to describe its problem, but automatically conditioning every policy hypothetical on “the box is full” would eliminate the predictor-sensitive comparison. The construction here treats the observation as an input applied to the selected policy, rather than as an event held fixed in every hypothetical.

That is an interpretation, not a complete rule for every kind of evidence. In particular, background evidence that does not depend on the policy may appropriately inform the model. Distinguishing it from policy-dependent evidence can itself require a substantive dependency analysis.

## 5. Policy entries and other agents

The paper suggests that the policy/action distinction can matter in multi-agent dilemmas, without giving a general worked-out policy rule. Consider the following illustrative model.

Two players receive distinct role inputs, \(L\) and \(R\), and both apply the **same selected policy** \(\pi\). Each can cooperate, \(C\), or defect, \(D\). Player \(L\)’s payoff is

\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & R:C & R:D\\ \hline
L:C & 3 & 0\\
L:D & 4 & 1
\end{array}
\]

The four candidate policies yield player \(L\):

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\pi(L)&\pi(R)&U_L\\ \hline
C&C&3\\
C&D&0\\
D&C&4\\
D&D&1
\end{array}
\]

If P-FDT evaluates these policies solely by \(L\)’s utility, it selects \(\pi(L)=D,\pi(R)=C\), giving \(L\) payoff 4. This is not a recommendation that every pair of P-FDT agents achieves mutual cooperation. It follows only because the model assumes a shared policy selector whose output controls both role-specific actions, and because the objective used to select that shared policy is \(L\)’s payoff.

Change those assumptions and the answer changes. If the players have separate selectors, one player’s policy intervention need not change the other’s policy. If the selector instead maximizes a symmetric sum of utilities, a different policy may win or tie. If both players run nominally similar algorithms but have different inputs and objectives, their computations may not be identical.

By contrast, in the paper’s psychological-twin prisoner’s dilemma, the two agents reason identically on effectively the same relevant input. Intervening on their common decision output links their actions: cooperation gives each \$1,000,000, while defection gives each \$1,000. Under that dependency model, P-FDT cooperates. The role-input example shows why “the agents use the same theory” is not, by itself, enough to identify the dependency.

For unfamiliar games, the practical questions are therefore precise: Which computation is shared? Which inputs differ? Which policy entries does each implementation apply? Whose utility is optimized? A policy intervention should change another agent’s behavior only to the extent supported by the answers.

## 6. P-FDT and predictable threats

Consider the paper’s mechanical blackmail: a blackmailer starts a program that will cause \$1,000,000 in damage unless the recipient pays \$1,000. The blackmailer starts it only when she predicts payment. Suppose, for a simple imperfect-prediction model, that:

- a policy of paying when threatened leads to a threat with probability \(1\), costing \$1,000;
- a policy of refusing leads to a mistaken threat with probability \(\varepsilon\), costing \$1,000,000 when it occurs.

Then the expected costs are

\[
C(\pi_{\mathrm{pay}})=1{,}000,
\qquad
C(\pi_{\mathrm{refuse}})=\varepsilon\,1{,}000{,}000.
\]

Under this model P-FDT refuses when \(\varepsilon<0.001\), and pays when \(\varepsilon>0.001\). At equality a tie-breaker is needed. If a threat is actually received under the refusal policy, refusing incurs the large loss; the policy is selected because its *overall hypothetical consequences* are better under the stipulated predictor response.

The numerical threshold also illustrates why assumptions matter. If the blackmailer threatens regardless of prediction, refusing has no deterrent benefit. If paying has other future consequences, or the blackmailer’s errors depend differently on each policy, those must enter the calculation.

The source’s numerical prose contains an apparent reversal: it says refusal is preferred at error probability \(0.0009\), but payment at \(0.011\%\). Since \(0.011\%=0.00011\), the latter gives an expected refusal loss of only \$110 under the stated simplified assumptions, not more than \$1,000. The threshold calculation, rather than that sentence’s prescription, governs this model.

## 7. What is settled, and what remains open?

The paper motivates functional reasoning partly by comparison with precommitment. Before meeting a predictor, an agent might welcome a binding commitment to one-box or pay a driver after rescue. P-FDT seeks to produce the corresponding behavior without a separate physical commitment. This is a motivation, not a general theorem: a policy selected under one model need not coincide with every precommitment an agent would choose under different information or constraints.

Likewise, the paper argues that functional agents need not assign negative value to information, because a policy can ignore an observation. A standard policy-space argument supports a limited claim. If acquiring information is free, has no other effect on the environment, and the expanded policy class includes every old policy that ignores it, then

\[
\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{informed}}}V(\pi)
\geq
\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{uninformed}}}V(\pi).
\]

But those assumptions are not automatic. An adversary might observe that information was acquired, or the acquisition might alter which computation is predicted. Then “ignore the information” need not reproduce the old situation. The broader claim requires a model of those dependencies.

Several implementation questions remain open:

1. **Policy scope.** Does the output cover one decision, an entire lifetime, or a family of agents? A larger scope can expose valuable coordination but also introduce irrelevant or incompatible cases.
2. **Observation and updating.** What information is built into the model before policy evaluation, and what is treated as a policy input? Moving evidence across that boundary may change the answer.
3. **Logical dependence.** How should an agent infer partial dependence between its computation and an imperfect predictor or similar algorithm? Ordinary statistical fit is evidence, but does not uniquely determine the functional intervention.
4. **Randomness.** A predictor may know a distribution without knowing a future genuinely random sample; copied pseudorandom generators may instead produce linked samples. The model must distinguish these cases.
5. **Self-reference and computation.** A selector can represent its own output without already knowing that output, but self-reference does not by itself guarantee that optimization is tractable, that a maximum exists, or that the proposed dependency graph is correct.

The source claims strong performance in a range of intuitive “fair” problems, while acknowledging that neither fairness nor general optimality has been formally established. Any theory can be favored or punished by a specially constructed environment. P-FDT’s substantive bet is narrower and more interesting: in environments where predictions and shared computations matter, respecting those dependencies can lead to better decisions than either conditioning on actions indiscriminately or considering only their physical effects.

## Conclusion

P-FDT evaluates candidate **policies** as alternative outputs of a decision computation, propagates each through modeled causal and computational dependencies, selects the policy with highest hypothetical expected utility, and applies it to the actual input. Its distinctive lesson is that the consequences of a decision rule can include predictions and other implementations—not merely the local act it produces.

The policy-selection footnote supplies the direction, not a finished universal algorithm. Using P-FDT well therefore requires making the policy class, evidence boundary, predictor response, and shared computations explicit. The result is a powerful framework for analyzing decision problems, alongside an unfinished theory of the counterfactual dependencies on which its conclusions depend.
