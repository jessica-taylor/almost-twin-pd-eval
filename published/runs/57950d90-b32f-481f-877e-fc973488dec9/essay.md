# Causal Decision Theory: Choosing What to Change

A decision can be informative without being effective. Taking a medicine may indicate that someone is seriously ill, even when the medicine improves their prospects. Conversely, declining treatment may be good news about someone’s health without making them healthier.

Causal decision theory (CDT) builds its account of rational choice around this distinction. It recommends actions with the best expected **causal consequences**, rather than actions most strongly associated with desirable outcomes. The central question is:

> Given what I currently know, what would happen if I performed each available action?

This is not always equivalent to asking what I should expect upon learning which action I perform.

The reference article presents CDT through probabilities of counterfactuals and discusses both its intuitive appeal and its controversial recommendations. This essay develops that presentation into an operational framework. Structural causal models and policy interventions will supply additional mathematical machinery; they are useful implementations, not definitions mandated by the article.

## 1. Expected utility over counterfactual outcomes

Let \(\mathcal A\) be a set of available actions, \(E\) the agent’s present evidence, and \(u(o)\) the utility of a complete outcome \(o\). “Complete” matters: costs, risks, effects on others, and morally relevant features must be represented wherever the agent values them.

For each action \(a\), write \(O_a\) for the outcome that would occur if the agent performed \(a\). A standard formulation of CDT is

\[
V_C(a\mid E)
=
\mathbb E[u(O_a)\mid E]
=
\sum_o P(O_a=o\mid E)\,u(o).
\]

The recommendation is

\[
a^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in\mathcal A}V_C(a\mid E).
\]

The probabilities express uncertainty about what an action would produce. They need not express indeterminism: an outcome can be causally determined while the agent remains uncertain about it.

The article, following Gibbard and Harper, writes the evaluation as

\[
U(A)=\sum_j P(A\to O_j)D(O_j).
\]

Here the arrow should be understood counterfactually: roughly, “if \(A\) were performed, \(O_j\) would occur.” It is not ordinary material implication, nor must it mean that \(A\) logically necessitates \(O_j\). The important object is a probability assigned to a causal counterfactual.

By contrast, evidential decision theory evaluates

\[
V_E(a\mid E)
=
\sum_o P(O=o\mid A=a,E)\,u(o).
\]

EDT asks what the action would tell us; CDT asks what performing it would bring about.

Neither theory determines utility. CDT can evaluate selfish or altruistic preferences, monetary gains or health outcomes. Its distinctive commitment concerns the probabilities used to evaluate options.

## 2. Making the counterfactuals operational

CDT’s maximization rule is incomplete until we specify the counterfactual distributions. The reference article identifies this as a major source of controversy.

One operational approach uses a structural causal model. Suppose the world contains variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\), generated by equations

\[
X_i=f_i(\operatorname{Pa}_i,W_i),
\]

where \(\operatorname{Pa}_i\) are the causal parents of \(X_i\), and \(W_i\) represent background influences. Assume an acyclic model so these equations determine outcomes unambiguously.

Ordinarily, the action variable \(A\) also has an equation: actions arise from deliberation, habits, information, and circumstances. To evaluate choosing \(a\), replace that equation by

\[
A:=a,
\]

leaving the remaining equations intact. This is the intervention \(\operatorname{do}(A=a)\). In this implementation,

\[
V_C(a\mid E)
=
\sum_o P(o\mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),E)\,u(o).
\]

The intervention removes the usual causes of the action from its production. It does **not** remove the action’s effects.

For example, if treatment affects recovery, intervening on treatment changes the recovery distribution. If illness affects both treatment choice and recovery, intervening on treatment does not thereby change whether the patient was initially ill.

A useful calculation separates three operations:

1. **Update:** infer the background circumstances from current evidence.
2. **Intervene:** replace the action-generating equation.
3. **Predict:** propagate the consequences through the remaining equations.

If the causal model itself is uncertain, let \(K\) index candidate models and \(W\) their background variables. Then

\[
V_C(a\mid E)
=
\sum_k\int
u\!\left(O^{k}_a(w)\right)
p(k,w\mid E)\,dw.
\]

The same posterior distribution \(p(k,w\mid E)\) is used for every candidate action. What changes is the outcome generated under that action.

This captures a central CDT commitment: merely *supposing* that one performs \(a\) does not automatically supply new evidence about which background model is actual. Genuine new information can change the posterior; an intervention considered during deliberation is not itself an observation.

The article also discusses Lewis’s alternative technique of **imaging**. Probability is transferred from each possible world to a suitably close world where the action occurs. Structural interventions similarly specify which features remain fixed and which change. Neither method eliminates the substantive question: what makes a counterfactual world the appropriate comparison?

## 3. Correlation, common causes, and agreement with EDT

Consider a simplified version of the article’s King David example. David wants to summon Bathsheba. He believes that an unchangeable personality trait influences both his inclination to act unjustly and his vulnerability to revolt. Crucially, the stipulated unjust act does not itself cause revolt.

The causal structure is

\[
T\longrightarrow A,
\qquad
T\longrightarrow R,
\]

where \(T\) is the trait, \(A\) the action, and \(R\) revolt. There is no arrow \(A\to R\).

Consequently,

\[
P(R\mid A=a,E)
=
\sum_t P(R\mid t,E)P(t\mid A=a,E)
\]

can vary with \(a\): the action supplies evidence about David’s trait. But

\[
P(R\mid\operatorname{do}(A=a),E)
=
\sum_t P(R\mid t,E)P(t\mid E)
\]

does not vary with \(a\).

For a numerical illustration, suppose the unfavorable trait has probability \(1/2\). Revolt probabilities are \(0.8\) with that trait and \(0.1\) otherwise. Summoning provides utility \(10\), while revolt costs \(100\). Then CDT assigns

\[
V_C(\text{summon})=10-100(0.45)=-35,
\]

\[
V_C(\text{abstain})=-100(0.45)=-45.
\]

It recommends summoning, given these stipulated utilities and causal assumptions.

That is not a moral endorsement. If the injustice itself carries sufficient negative utility, or if summoning actually increases revolt risk, the recommendation changes.

CDT and EDT agree when observational and interventional probabilities coincide. In potential-outcome notation, sufficient conditions are consistency,

\[
A=a\implies O=O_a,
\]

and conditional independence,

\[
O_a\perp A\mid E.
\]

For actions with positive observational probability, these yield

\[
P(O_a=o\mid E)=P(O=o\mid A=a,E).
\]

Thus disagreement is not inevitable. It arises when the action carries information about outcome-relevant circumstances that performing the action would not change.

The article discusses related independence conditions for counterfactuals. It also invokes Lewis’s work warning against identifying probabilities of conditionals with conditional probabilities as a general rule.

## 4. Newcomb’s problem: a fixed past and a predictive correlation

In Newcomb’s problem, a transparent box contains \(\$1{,}000\). An opaque box contains either \(\$1{,}000{,}000\) or nothing. A predictor has already filled the opaque box if it predicted that the player would take only that box, and left it empty otherwise.

Let \(B\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether the opaque box is full. The payoffs are

\[
u(\text{one},B)=10^6B,
\]

\[
u(\text{two},B)=10^6B+1000.
\]

Under the usual causal interpretation, the present choice does not affect the past filling. Writing \(q=P(B=1\mid E)\),

\[
V_C(\text{one})=10^6q,
\qquad
V_C(\text{two})=10^6q+1000.
\]

CDT therefore recommends taking both boxes.

The argument does not deny the predictor’s reliability. It says that reliability creates an evidential relationship, not a causal route from the current action to the already established contents.

For comparison, suppose the predictive probabilities are symmetrically

\[
P(B=1\mid \text{one},E)=0.99,
\qquad
P(B=1\mid \text{two},E)=0.01.
\]

Then EDT values one-boxing at \(\$990{,}000\), and two-boxing at \(\$11{,}000\).

These conditional probabilities are an additional specification: an overall accuracy rate of \(99\%\) alone does not establish them.

Newcomb’s problem exposes competing standards of comparison. CDT compares actions against the same uncertain box contents. EDT compares the different distributions of contents associated with those actions. The observation that one-boxers generally become richer is therefore not something CDT overlooks; its normative significance is precisely what is disputed.

## 5. Policies, commitments, and randomization

A policy specifies actions as a function of information. If \(H_t\) is the information available at time \(t\), a deterministic policy has the form

\[
A_t=\pi_t(H_t).
\]

A natural extension of the intervention framework evaluates

\[
V_C(\pi\mid E)
=
\mathbb E\!\left[u(O)\mid\operatorname{do}(\pi),E\right],
\]

where the relevant action-generating mechanisms are replaced by the policy.

This extension requires care about **when and how the policy is selected**. Suppose, before Newcomb’s predictor makes its prediction, the agent can install an observable, binding one-boxing mechanism. If installation causally influences the prediction, CDT can recommend installing it. Later, without such a commitment, CDT can still recommend taking both boxes once the contents are fixed.

These are different decision problems. A present choice cannot acquire causal influence over the past merely by being redescribed as “choosing a policy.” The proposed policy must be an available intervention with the stated effects.

The same distinction matters in games. In a one-shot prisoner’s dilemma, defection may causally dominate cooperation if the other player’s action is unaffected by one’s own. A strong correlation with a psychologically similar “twin” does not, by itself, create causal influence. But communication, enforceable commitments, and repeated interaction can create genuine causal advantages to cooperation.

Randomization also needs a physical interpretation. If an independent randomizer selects \(a\) with probability \(\mu(a)\), and nothing responds differently to the randomization procedure, then

\[
V_C(\mu\mid E)=\sum_a\mu(a)V_C(a\mid E).
\]

This is a weighted average, so mixing cannot outperform the best pure action. Randomization can matter when it changes opponents’ information, predictions, or behavior. Those effects must be modeled rather than assumed away.

## 6. Self-knowledge and unstable deliberation

The most difficult criticisms concern actions that reveal information about the chooser.

### The psychopath button

In the article’s example, Paul can press a button that kills all psychopaths. He values a world without psychopaths, but strongly prefers survival. He also believes that only a psychopath would press.

Let \(T\) mean that Paul is a psychopath, and let

\[
q=P(T\mid E).
\]

Relative to not pressing, suppose pressing gives net utility \(g>0\) if he is not a psychopath and \(-L\) if he is. A straightforward fixed-belief CDT calculation gives

\[
V_C(\text{press})=(1-q)g-qL.
\]

It recommends pressing whenever

\[
q<\frac{g}{g+L}.
\]

Yet learning that Paul presses might make his being a psychopath overwhelmingly likely.

The difficulty is not that CDT ignores the causal danger. The calculation includes death when Paul is a psychopath. Rather, it uses his **current probability** of that condition instead of the probability conditional on pressing.

The article reports Joyce’s reply that CDT can incorporate information about what one is inclined or likely to do. If Paul discovers a strong inclination to press, that discovery can raise \(q\) before the intervention is evaluated.

A reasonable operational interpretation is therefore:

- update on genuine evidence about one’s dispositions;
- evaluate interventions using the resulting beliefs;
- reconsider if deliberation itself produces further evidence.

But this is not a complete solution. We still need an account of which deliberative states constitute evidence and whether the process reaches a stable recommendation. Automatically replacing \(P(T\mid E)\) by \(P(T\mid A=a,E)\) for every hypothetical action would abandon the distinction CDT was introduced to preserve.

### Death in Damascus

Suppose Death predicts whether you will stay in Damascus or travel elsewhere, then waits at the predicted location.

If your present probability that Death is in Damascus is \(p\), CDT assigns survival probabilities

\[
P(\text{survive}\mid\operatorname{do}(\text{stay}),E)=1-p,
\]

\[
P(\text{survive}\mid\operatorname{do}(\text{leave}),E)=p.
\]

So you prefer leaving when \(p>1/2\). But becoming convinced that you will leave may itself be evidence that Death waits elsewhere, reversing the preference.

CDT can represent the predictive correlation. The challenge is explaining how a deliberator should manage the interaction between causal evaluation and changing self-expectations. A fixed-belief maximization rule alone does not supply that dynamics.

### The adversarial offer

The article also describes two boxes costing \(\$1\) each, of which the buyer may purchase at most one. Yesterday, a seller placed \(\$3\) in every box she predicted the buyer would not acquire. Her prediction is believed to be \(75\%\) accurate.

At least one box contains \(\$3\). Thus the sum of their expected contents is at least \(\$3\), so one box has expected contents of at least \(\$1.50\). Holding beliefs about those already fixed contents constant, CDT recommends buying a best box rather than declining.

Nevertheless, for a buyer whose acquired box is predicted correctly with probability \(0.75\), the actual expected net payoff is

\[
0.75(-1)+0.25(2)=-0.25.
\]

This illustrates the criticism that causal evaluation can recommend participation in predictably unfavorable arrangements. The formal issue is again the difference between fixed-background action comparisons and distributions correlated with the action ultimately selected. The article reports related money-pump and diachronic Dutch-book objections; these remain disputed rather than settled refutations.

## 7. Applying CDT to unfamiliar problems

A disciplined application should make five things explicit.

**First, specify the decision point.** What can the agent change now? Earlier commitments and later choices are distinct interventions.

**Second, specify utilities over complete consequences.** Money is not automatically utility, and causal reasoning cannot compensate for omitted harms.

**Third, draw the dependencies.** Separate causal arrows from correlations generated by common causes, prediction, or shared reasoning.

**Fourth, construct intervention distributions.** Update on available evidence, alter the selected action mechanism, and propagate its effects. Do not freeze genuine downstream consequences.

**Fifth, check robustness and self-consistency.** Would a different plausible causal model reverse the recommendation? Does new information about the agent’s own deliberation require updating? Is a proposed policy genuinely implementable?

Where causal structure is uncertain, averaging over explicit models is preferable to silently treating one controversial counterfactual as certain.

## Conclusion

CDT’s central discipline is to distinguish changing the world from learning about it. Its calculations become precise once utilities, evidence, causal dependencies, and available interventions are specified. Its hardest questions arise when actions predict hidden circumstances or deliberation reveals the agent’s own type. In those cases, causal modeling clarifies the disagreement, but does not by itself settle how rational choice should reconcile intervention, self-knowledge, and predictable success.
