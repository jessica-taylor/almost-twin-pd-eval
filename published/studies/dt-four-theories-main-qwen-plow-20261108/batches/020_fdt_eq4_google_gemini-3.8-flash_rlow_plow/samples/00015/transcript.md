# Action Functional Decision Theory (A-FDT): Mathematical Foundations, Subjunctive Dependence, and Algorithmic Choice

Traditional normative decision theory has long been divided between two paradigms: **Evidential Decision Theory (EDT)** and **Causal Decision Theory (CDT)**. EDT advises an agent to choose an action that serves as the best epistemic evidence for desirable outcomes, computing expectations via standard Bayesian conditionalization. CDT corrects for EDT's notorious tendency to "manage the news" in common-cause scenarios by restricting evaluation to the physical consequences directly downstream of the agent's physical bodily action, formalized using causal interventions such as Pearl's $\Do$-operator.

Yet both theories exhibit systematic failures. CDT two-boxes in Newcomb's problem, walks away empty-handed from transparent boxes, defects against psychological twins, and is left to die in Parfit's desert—sacrificing immense utility to preserve an intuitive but narrow physical-causal conception of dominance. EDT one-boxes in Newcomb's problem but refrains in the Smoking Lesion dilemma, succumbs to blackmail schemes (such as the XOR Blackmail), and falls prey to severe dynamic pathology when conditioned on extreme priors.

**Action Functional Decision Theory (A-FDT)**—formalized in Equation (4), Section 5 of Yudkowsky and Soares (2017)—resolves this impasse. Rather than intervening on physical action tokens (as in CDT) or passively conditioning on the event that an action occurred (as in EDT), A-FDT evaluates the hypothetical consequences of the *output of the agent's mathematical decision function*. 

This essay provides a rigorous mathematical exposition of A-FDT. We present its core formal architecture, contrast its treatment of subjunctive counterfactuals with those of CDT and EDT, examine its mechanics across classic decision dilemmas, and identify the formal and foundational questions that remain open.

## 1. The Decision-Theoretic Landscape

Let $\mathcal{A}$ denote a finite set of available actions, $\mathcal{X}$ a countable set of observation histories, and $\mathcal{O}$ a countable set of possible outcomes. A utility function $\Util: \mathcal{O} \to \mathbb{R}$ maps outcomes to real numbers, bounded such that all expectations are well-defined and finite. We denote the utility random variable by $\VV \coloneqq \Util(\Outcome)$.

Following Gibbard and Harper (1978) and Joyce (1999), rational choice can be cast as the maximization of expected utility under a hypothetical conditional connective "$\then$":
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{EU}(a) \coloneqq \sum_{j=1}^N P(a \then o_j ;\, x) \, \Util(o_j), \label{eq:umax}
\end{equation}
where $P(a \then o_j ;\, x)$ denotes the subjective probability that outcome $o_j$ would obtain were the agent to choose action $a$ after observing history $x \in \mathcal{X}$. Joyce's representation theorem demonstrates that agents obeying core axioms of conditional preference will necessarily maximize an expected utility functional of this form. The foundational dispute between rival decision theories does not concern the axioms of value, but rather the *epistemic and counterfactual perspective* that defines $P(a \then o_j ;\, x)$.

### Evidential Decision Theory (EDT)
EDT identifies the hypothetical conditional with conditional probability: $P(a \then o_j ;\, x) \coloneqq P(o_j \mid x, a)$. The EDT decision rule is:
\begin{equation}
\EDT(P, x) \coloneqq \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \EE\left(\VV \mid \Obs = x, \, \Act = a\right). \label{eq:edt}
\end{equation}
By conditioning directly on $\Act = a$, EDT preserves *all* correlations between the agent's action and environmental variables, regardless of whether those correlations are causal, logical, or purely accidental.

### Causal Decision Theory (CDT)
CDT asserts that counterfactuals must reflect physical-causal mechanisms. Utilizing Pearl’s causal graphical framework (Pearl 2000), the world-model is a pair $(P, G)$, where $G$ is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) whose directed edges represent direct physical-causal mechanisms. The hypothetical is generated by the surgical intervention operator $\Do(\Act = a)$, which severs all incoming edges to $\Act$ in $G$ while leaving invariant the conditional probability distributions of non-descendants:
\begin{equation}
\CDT(P, G, x) \coloneqq \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \EE\left(\VV \cond \Do(\Act = a), \, \Obs = x\right). \label{eq:cdt}
\end{equation}
CDT severs all correlations between $\Act$ and variables that are not causally downstream from the physical act, treating them as strictly invariant under counterfactual evaluation.

## 2. Action Functional Decision Theory (A-FDT): Mathematical Formulation

A-FDT rejects the assumption that the fundamental object of rational evaluation is the *physical token* of an action. Instead, an A-FDT agent conceptualizes her deliberation as the local execution of an invariant mathematical function. 

### The Core Formalism
Let the agent’s decision process be governed by a fixed mathematical function $\FDT$. The agent maintains a world-model $(P, G)$, where:
1. $P$ is a subjective probability distribution over states of the world. Crucially, $P$ contains random variables representing the outputs of mathematical, computational, and logical functions.
2. $G$ is an augmented dependency graph. Unlike Pearl’s standard causal graphs, which encode purely physical mechanisms, $G$ encodes *subjunctive dependencies*—including both physical causal paths and logical-mathematical entailments.
3. $x \in \mathcal{X}$ is the observation history input to the agent.

The primary formal presentation of A-FDT (Equation 4, Section 5 of Yudkowsky and Soares 2017) defines the action rule as:
\begin{equation}
\FDT(P, G, x) \coloneqq \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \EE\left(\VV \cond \Do\left(\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x}) = a\right)\right). \label{eq:fdt}
\end{equation}

Here, $\FDTvar$ is a formal symbol within the agent's model denoting the mathematical function that the agent runs. The underlines indicate *dequoting* (syntactic reflection): if $x = \text{"history\_1"}$, then $\underline{x}$ is the literal syntactic representation of that token inside the model. Thus, $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$ is a node in $G$ denoting the logical proposition:
$$\text{"The output of mathematical procedure } \FDT \text{ evaluated on input tuple } (P, G, x) \text{ is } a\text{."}$$

### Algorithmic Architecture
Equation \eqref{eq:fdt} operates as an algorithmic procedure:
1. **Model Construction**: The agent encodes her background knowledge into a joint probability distribution $P$ over a set of variables, and constructs a graph $G$ that captures causal paths as well as algorithmic-logical dependencies.
2. **Self-Reference via Quining**: The model $P$ contains an un-evaluated functional node $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$. By Kleene’s Second Recursion Theorem, an algorithm can manipulate and refer to its own code and inputs without circular non-termination.
3. **Hypothetical Evaluation via Graph Surgery**: For each candidate action $a \in \mathcal{A}$, the agent applies Pearl’s $\Do$-operator not to the physical act node $\Act$, but to the logical variable $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$. The operator $\Do(\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x}) = a)$ severs incoming arrows to that logical node in $G$ and propagates the assignment forward along all directed paths.
4. **Action Selection**: The agent computes the mathematical expectation of utility under each post-intervention distribution and outputs the maximizing action.

### Two Critical Structural Divergences from CDT
Comparing Equation \eqref{eq:cdt} and Equation \eqref{eq:fdt} illuminates two core mathematical distinctions:

1. **Type Intervention vs. Token Intervention**: CDT intervenes on $\Act = a$, a physical variable representing whether bodily effectors fire. A-FDT intervenes on $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x}) = a$, a mathematical type. If an external system (such as an accurate predictor or psychological twin) runs, simulates, or tracks this same function, that system's state lies *downstream of the functional node in $G$*. Consequently, the intervention alters the predicted state in the agent's hypothetical.
2. **Dynamic Update via Variable Substitution, Not Bayesian Conditioning**: When a CDT agent receives new observations $x \to y$, she updates her distribution via standard Bayesian conditioning: $P(- \mid \Obs = y)$. An A-FDT agent, however, responds to an observation $y$ by *changing the site of intervention* from $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$ to $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{y})$. As shown in Section 4, this prevents the agent from falling into dynamic inconsistencies or suffering exploitation in transparent scenarios.

## 3. Subjunctive Dependence vs. Causal and Statistical Dependence

To apply Equation \eqref{eq:fdt} to unfamiliar cases, one must understand how $G$ is structured. A-FDT establishes an explicit hierarchy of dependence:

$$\text{Causal Dependence} \subset \text{Subjunctive Dependence} \subset \text{Statistical Correlation}$$

```
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
| Statistical Correlation (EDT)                               |
|   e.g., Common causes, genetic lesions, spurious correlations|

|  | Subjunctive Dependence (A-FDT)                        |  |
|  |   Shared mathematical algorithms, simulations, twins   |  |

|  |  | Physical-Causal Dependence (CDT)                |  |  |
|  |  |   Forward-time physical signals, effector force |  |  |
|  |  +-------------------------------------------------+  |  |
|  +-------------------------------------------------------+  |
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### The Calculator Analogy
Consider two calibrated calculators programmed to compute $f(z) = 6288 + z$. Calculator 1 is in Paris; Calculator 2 is in Tokyo. There is no physical communication between them. Yet, if one observes Calculator 1 outputting $7336$ when $z = 1048$, one can be certain that Calculator 2 will also output $7336$. 

* **CDT View**: Intervening on the physical wiring of Calculator 1 to force a display of $3159$ has zero physical effect on Calculator 2. CDT concludes the two machines are counterfactually independent.
* **A-FDT View**: The physical displays of both machines *subjunctively depend* on the mathematical output of the function $f(1048)$. If an agent asks, "What would Calculator 2 display if $f(1048)$ evaluated to $3159$?", the correct answer is that Calculator 2 would display $3159$. Calculator 2's display is functionally downstream of $f$.

### The Mechanics of Subjunctive Dependencies
A variable $Y$ subjunctively depends on a decision function node $F$ if the physical state of $Y$ is determined by a process that runs, approximates, or embeds an accurate representation of $F$. 
* **Predictors and Simulations**: If an entity $E$ predicts an agent's choice by simulating her code or reasoning, $E$'s prediction node $\Var{Pred}$ is a child of $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$ in $G$.
* **Psychological Twins**: If another agent $T$ executes the identical decision procedure on symmetric inputs, $T$'s action node $\Act_T$ is a child of the same functional node.
* **Lesions and Merely Correlated Traits**: If an internal biological lesion causes both cancer and an urge to smoke, the cancer's occurrence is biologically fixed. Cancer does not simulate or query the agent's decision algorithm; hence, $\Var{Cancer}$ is *not* a child of the functional node $\FDTvar$ in $G$.

### 4.1 Newcomb’s Problem
An agent faces two boxes: Box A (transparent, containing \$1,000) and Box B (opaque, containing \$1,000,000 or \$0). A reliable predictor placed \$1,000,000 in Box B iff she predicted the agent would forgo Box A. The prediction has already occurred.

```
       [ fdt(P, G, x) ]  <-- A-FDT intervention point
          /          \
         v            v
      [ Act ]     [ Prediction ]
         \            /
          v          v
          [ Outcome / V ]
```

* **CDT Evaluation**: CDT intervenes on $\Act$. The causal graph has $\Act \to \Outcome \leftarrow \Var{Box B}$, with $\Var{Prediction} \to \Var{Box B}$. Because $\Var{Box B}$ is not downstream of $\Act$, $\Do(\Act = a)$ leaves the probability distribution over $\Var{Box B}$ invariant:
  $$\EE[\VV \mid \Do(\Act = \Val{twobox})] = \EE[\VV \mid \Do(\Act = \Val{onebox})] + 1000.$$
  CDT two-boxes and walks away with \$1,000.
* **A-FDT Evaluation**: In Fiona’s (the A-FDT agent) graph $G_F$, the node $F \coloneqq \FDTvar(\underline{P_F}, \underline{G_F}, \underline{\emptyset})$ has directed edges to both $\Act$ and $\Var{Prediction}$, because the predictor modeled $F$:
  $$\Act \coloneqq F, \quad \Var{Prediction} \coloneqq F \text{ (with probability } 0.99\text{)}.$$
  Evaluating Equation \eqref{eq:fdt}:
  $$\EE\left(\VV \cond \Do(F = \Val{onebox})\right) = 0.99(\$1,000,000) + 0.01(\$0) = \$990,000,$$
  $$\EE\left(\VV \cond \Do(F = \Val{twobox})\right) = 0.01(\$1,001,000) + 0.99(\$1,000) = \$11,000.$$
  Equation \eqref{eq:fdt} yields $\argmax_a \EE = \Val{onebox}$. A-FDT one-boxes and secures \$1,000,000.

### 4.2 The Smoking Lesion Problem
Smoking is strongly correlated with fatal lung cancer, but only through a common genetic lesion that predisposes individuals to enjoy smoking and independently causes cancer. Smoking does not physically cause cancer.

* **EDT Evaluation**: Conditioning on $\Act = \Val{smoke}$ raises the posterior probability of having the fatal lesion. EDT irrationally refrains from smoking, sacrificing \$1,000 of utility to purchase "good news" about its biology.
* **A-FDT Evaluation**: In A-FDT’s graph, $\Var{Lesion} \to \Var{Cancer}$ and $\Var{Lesion} \to \Act$. Crucially, $\Var{Cancer}$ is **not** a child of $\FDTvar$, because cancer does not instantiate or track the agent's decision logic. Thus:
  $$P(\Var{Lesion} \mid \Do(\FDTvar = \Val{smoke})) = P(\Var{Lesion} \mid \Do(\FDTvar = \Val{refrain})) = P(\Var{Lesion}).$$
  Intervening on the functional decision node breaks the statistical correlation with the lesion just as Pearl's $\Do$ breaks it in CDT. A-FDT smokes, achieving maximal expected utility without managing the news.

* **A-FDT Evaluation**: A-FDT updates not by conditioning its probability distribution to collapse the world, but by shifting its intervention site to $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \Val{full})$. The graph $G$ reflects that the predictor's past action depended upon the output of the function when fed the input $\Val{full}$:
  $$\Var{Prediction} = \text{Predict}\left(\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \Val{full})\right).$$
  When evaluating $\Do(\FDTvar(\dots, \Val{full}) = \Val{twobox})$, the surgery propagates through the graph to the predictor's past node, setting $\Var{Prediction} = \Val{empty}$ (with 99% probability). Under that hypothetical, Box B would not have been filled in the first place!

  $$\EE[\VV \mid \Do(\FDTvar(\dots, \Val{full}) = \Val{onebox})] \approx \$1,000,000,$$

## 6. Open Problems and Foundational Uncertainties

While A-FDT delivers superior outcomes across predictive dilemmas, the paper acknowledges that its mathematical and philosophical foundations leave several open questions. Here, we distinguish what is established in the source from the additional machinery required to operationalize it.

### 6.1 The Counterpossible Problem and the $\True$ Operator
The most severe conceptual hurdle for A-FDT is the formal status of **counterpossibles**—counterfactual conditionals with logically impossible antecedents (Cohen 1990; Bjerring 2013).

If $\FDT(P, G, x)$ is a deterministic mathematical function, it outputs a unique action $a^* \in \mathcal{A}$ as a matter of mathematical necessity. Evaluating alternative actions $a \neq a^*$ requires evaluating the hypothetical:
$$\text{"What would obtain if the mathematical function } \FDT(P, G, x) \text{ evaluated to } a \neq a^*\text{?"}$$

This antecedent is a mathematical contradiction, analogous to asking: "What would happen to elliptic curves if $7336 = 7337$?" In classical logic and probability theory, conditioning on a falsehood collapses the system via the principle of explosion. 

The paper proposes two ways to handle this:
1. **The Ideal Semantic Operator ($\True$)**: Define an operator $\True(\phi)$ that shifts probability mass over impossible logical states without mathematical collapse (e.g., via non-standard epistemic models or logical priors; Gaifman 2004; Demski 2012). The authors note that no complete, tractable formalization of $\True$ exists.
2. **The Pragmatic Causal Graph Workaround (Equation 4)**: Yudkowsky and Soares bypass the counterpossible dilemma by treating $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$ simply as a formal random variable in a Pearl DAG, decoupling its logical necessity from its formal graph dynamics. Pearl’s $\Do$-operator mechanically wipes out the structural equations feeding into the variable, permitting assignment to $a$ without formal logical inconsistency.

*Critical Assessment*: Equation \eqref{eq:fdt} avoids mathematical collapse through a formal trick, but it imports an unmodeled assumption: the graph $G$ must already know which variables in the environment share logical dependencies with $\FDTvar$.

### 6.2 The Graph Discovery Problem for Subjunctive Dependencies
In standard CDT, Pearl (2000) provides an operational methodology for deducing causal graphs $G$ from empirical observations and active physical interventions (e.g., conditional independence tests, instrumental variables, randomized controlled trials).

In A-FDT, **no such formal discovery algorithm exists**. How does an agent learn from data that a predictor in the desert is running a simulation of her code, whereas a carcinogenic lesion is not? 
* Can an agent physically intervene on a mathematical truth to test for independence? No; one cannot randomize the output of a mathematical function across experimental trials.
* The source admits that learning subjunctive dependence structures from data is "perhaps the largest open problem in the study of functional decision theory."

### 6.3 Proposal for an Operational Interpretation: Program-Trace Subsumption
To operationalize graph construction for A-FDT without requiring a full theory of counterpossibles, we propose a concrete computational interpretation based on **Program-Trace Subsumption**:

> **Definition (Subjunctive Parent Criterion)**: Let an environment contain physical processes $Y_1, \dots, Y_m$, each simulated by a Turing machine. An environmental variable $Y_k$ is a subjunctive child of an agent's decision node $\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x})$ iff the computational execution trace of $Y_k$ contains an explicit call to, or an exact emulation of, the algorithmic specification of $\FDT$.

Under this criterion:
* **Predictors**: The predictor computes $Y = \text{Simulate}(\text{AgentCode}, x)$. The computation trace of $Y$ directly evaluates the agent’s algorithm. Hence, an edge $\FDTvar \to Y$ is drawn in $G$.
* **Psychological Twins**: The twin computes $\Act_{\text{twin}} = \text{AgentCode}(x)$. The trace is identical. Hence, an edge $\FDTvar \to \Act_{\text{twin}}$ is drawn.
* **Smoking Lesion**: The biological lesion's causal mechanism consists of cellular metabolic processes. Its execution trace contains no simulation or evaluation of the agent’s deliberation. Hence, no edge $\FDTvar \to \Var{Cancer}$ exists.

This operational proposal grounds the construction of $G$ in computational recursion theory rather than metaphysical counterpossibles, providing an explicit recipe for implementing A-FDT agents in software.

## 7. A-FDT's Alternative Dominance Principle

Causal decision theorists frequently justify two-boxing in Newcomb's problem by appealing to the **Principle of Dominance**: if action $a_1$ yields a strictly better outcome than $a_2$ across every possible state of the world, $a_2$ is irrational. Carl the CDT agent argues:
* State 1 (Box B is full): Two-boxing yields \$1,001,000; one-boxing yields \$1,000,000.
* State 2 (Box B is empty): Two-boxing yields \$1,000; one-boxing yields \$0.
* Since $\$1,001,000 > \$1,000,000$ and $\$1,000 > \$0$, two-boxing dominates.

A-FDT exposes this argument as circular. The dominance principle relies entirely on what one holds fixed in defining the "states of the world":
* CDT defines states by holding fixed everything that is physically non-descendant from the action token $\Act$. This forces the predictor’s prediction to be held constant while varying the action, which corresponds to the counterfactual assumption that a 99% reliable predictor inexplicably fails.
* A-FDT defines states by holding fixed all variables that do not *subjunctively depend* on the decision function type $\FDTvar$. In Newcomb's problem, the content of Box B subjunctively depends on $\FDTvar$; hence, it cannot be held constant across the hypothetical comparison.

Under A-FDT’s subjunctive dominance principle:
* If the function outputs $\Val{onebox}$, the state of the world includes a full box with 99% probability, yielding $\approx \$990,000$.
* If the function outputs $\Val{twobox}$, the state of the world includes an empty box with 99% probability, yielding $\approx \$11,000$.

One-boxing subjunctively dominates two-boxing. As Yudkowsky and Soares emphasize, every expected utility theory induces its own internal concept of dominance. Dominance cannot serve as a neutral criterion for adjudicating between decision theories; normative superiority must ultimately be evaluated by the **utility achieved in practice**.

## 8. Conclusion

Action Functional Decision Theory (A-FDT) provides a mathematically precise and unified framework for rational choice. By shifting the locus of counterfactual intervention from the **physical action token** (CDT) to the **mathematical decision function type** (Equation 4):

$$\FDT(P, G, x) \coloneqq \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \EE\left(\VV \cond \Do\left(\FDTvar(\underline{P}, \underline{G}, \underline{x}) = a\right)\right),$$

A-FDT resolves the deep-seated pathologies of its predecessors. It one-boxes in Newcomb's problem, smokes in the Smoking Lesion dilemma, cooperates with psychological twins, escapes Parfit’s desert, and ignores the lures of extortionists and blackmailers. It achieves these outcomes without requiring ad-hoc precommitments, dynamic re-weighting, or the abandonment of expected utility maximization.

Crucial mathematical questions remain—most prominently, the formal foundations of counterpossible inference and the algorithmic extraction of subjunctive dependency graphs from observational data. Nevertheless, by aligning decision-theoretic hypotheticals with the invariant properties of mathematical computation, A-FDT establishes an important new foundation for the study of instrumental rationality and the engineering of advanced autonomous systems.
