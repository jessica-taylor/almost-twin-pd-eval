# Causal Decision Theory: Choosing Consequences, Not Evidence

Suppose an action is associated with good outcomes. Should you take it? That depends on why the association exists. A treatment might improve health, or healthy people might simply be more likely to receive it. An action might produce prosperity, or merely reveal that prosperity was already likely.

**Causal decision theory** (CDT) makes this distinction central. It recommends choosing the available action with the highest expected utility under the appropriate *causal counterfactual*: what would happen if you performed that action?

The supplied reference article presents this principle through counterfactual probabilities, contrasts it with evidential decision theory, and surveys challenges involving prediction and self-knowledge. This essay develops those ideas mathematically. Where the article leaves implementation unspecified, I introduce an intervention-based interpretation. That interpretation makes CDT operational in many ordinary cases, but it is an additional modeling framework—not a complete resolution of the philosophical disputes.

## 1. The basic decision rule

Let \(\mathcal A\) be a finite set of available actions, \(E\) the agent’s current evidence, and \(u(o)\) the utility of outcome \(o\). Outcomes should describe everything the agent values: benefits, costs, risks, effects on others, and any intrinsic value assigned to the action itself.

For each action \(a\), let \(O_a\) denote the outcome that would occur **if the agent performed \(a\)**. CDT recommends

\[
a^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in\mathcal A}
V_C(a\mid E),
\qquad
V_C(a\mid E)
=
\sum_o P(O_a=o\mid E)u(o).
\]

The reference gives Gibbard and Harper’s formulation:

\[
U(A)=\sum_j P(A\to O_j)D(O_j).
\]

Here the arrow should be understood counterfactually: “if \(A\) were performed, \(O_j\) would result.” It is not ordinary material implication, nor does it assert that \(A\) logically necessitates the outcome.

The probabilities represent uncertainty about which consequences an action would have. A treatment can be causally beneficial in expectation even when its effect on this particular patient is unknown.

Evidential decision theory (EDT), by contrast, evaluates

\[
V_E(a\mid E)
=
\sum_o P(O=o\mid A=a,E)u(o).
\]

EDT asks what outcomes to expect *upon learning that the action occurs*. CDT asks what outcomes to expect *under the counterfactual performance of the action*. These questions often agree, but they need not.

Neither theory supplies a utility function. In particular, CDT is not inherently selfish. A causal decision theorist who values other people’s welfare includes that welfare in \(u\). Conversely, a recommendation derived from a selfish utility function is not automatically a moral endorsement.

## 2. Making causal counterfactuals operational

The reference explicitly identifies an incompleteness: CDT does not itself provide a general algorithm for computing counterfactual probabilities. One useful operational interpretation uses a **structural causal model**.

Suppose the world is represented by equations

\[
X_i=f_i(\operatorname{Pa}_i,N_i),
\]

where \(\operatorname{Pa}_i\) are the causal parents of \(X_i\), and the \(N_i\) represent background circumstances. Assume, for simplicity, that the model is acyclic and its equations determine a unique outcome.

Ordinarily, the agent’s action is itself generated by some mechanism:

\[
A=f_A(\operatorname{Pa}_A,N_A).
\]

To evaluate action \(a\), replace this equation with

\[
A:=a,
\]

leaving the other mechanisms unchanged. This is an intervention, written \(\operatorname{do}(A=a)\). Recompute the downstream consequences.

For evidence available before the action, the resulting decision value is

\[
V_C(a\mid E)
=
\mathbb E[u(O)\mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),E].
\]

Equivalently, let \(\omega\) specify the background circumstances and \(O_a(\omega)\) the outcome obtained by solving the intervened model. Then

\[
V_C(a\mid E)
=
\int u(O_a(\omega))\,P(d\omega\mid E).
\]

The same posterior distribution over background circumstances is used for every candidate action. What changes is the action and its causal consequences—not the probability of a hidden cause merely because the hypothetical action would be evidence about it.

This does **not** mean freezing everything. Descendants of the action must change according to the model. If taking medicine changes blood pressure, blood pressure must be recomputed.

Causal uncertainty can also be represented. If \(M\) indexes competing models,

\[
V_C(a\mid E)
=
\sum_m P(M=m\mid E)
\mathbb E_m[u(O)\mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),E].
\]

Thus CDT need not assume certainty about causation.

The reference discusses another approach, Lewis’s *imaging*: probability mass at each possible world is transferred to a suitably closest world where the action occurs. This makes the unresolved question especially visible: what counts as “closest”? Structural equations provide one disciplined answer—preserve background circumstances and unaffected mechanisms—but only when those mechanisms and the intervention are adequately specified.

## 3. Why conditioning can mislead

Consider a hidden condition \(H\) that influences both treatment choice and health:

\[
H\longrightarrow A,
\qquad
H\longrightarrow O,
\qquad
A\longrightarrow O.
\]

Patients in worse condition might seek treatment more frequently. Consequently, treated patients could have worse outcomes even when treatment helps every patient.

Conditioning on treatment changes the inferred distribution of \(H\):

\[
P(O\mid A=a,E)
=
\sum_h
P(O\mid A=a,H=h,E)P(H=h\mid A=a,E).
\]

Intervening on treatment instead uses the pre-intervention distribution:

\[
P(O\mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),E)
=
\sum_h
P(O\mid A=a,H=h,E)P(H=h\mid E),
\]

provided \(H\) adequately controls for confounding in the assumed model.

The difference is not that CDT ignores information. It uses all information already possessed. It refuses to treat a *hypothetically selected action* as additional observational evidence about its own causes.

A sufficient condition for agreement between causal and evidential evaluation is conditional independence of the action and its potential outcomes:

\[
O_a\perp A\mid E
\]

for every \(a\), together with consistency and positive observational probability for the relevant action. Random assignment often creates this independence. Adequate observation of common causes can also establish it.

The article’s King David example has the same structure. A fixed personality trait influences both unjust behavior and the likelihood of revolt. Under the stipulated story, the unjust act itself does not cause revolt. The act therefore provides unfavorable evidence without producing that particular unfavorable consequence.

But this conclusion is limited by the stipulations. If injustice directly harms someone, carries moral disutility, or actually increases revolt risk, those effects belong in the causal evaluation. CDT does not license deleting inconvenient consequences from the model.

## 4. Newcomb’s problem: prediction without backward causation

Newcomb’s problem isolates a conflict between evidential association and causal dominance.

An opaque box contains either \(M=\$1{,}000{,}000\) or nothing. A transparent box contains \(k=\$1{,}000\). Earlier, a reliable predictor filled the opaque box exactly when it predicted that the player would take only that box.

Let \(B\in\{0,M\}\) be the already determined opaque-box contents. The available actions yield

\[
O_{\mathrm{one}}=B,
\qquad
O_{\mathrm{two}}=B+k.
\]

Assuming linear utility for money, ordinary forward-causal CDT gives

\[
V_C(\mathrm{two}\mid E)
-
V_C(\mathrm{one}\mid E)
=k.
\]

Both actions leave the earlier contents unchanged. Taking both boxes therefore gives an extra thousand dollars in every fixed content-state.

EDT conditions on the strong association between choice and prediction. Suppose, explicitly, that

\[
P(B=M\mid \mathrm{one},E)=0.99,
\qquad
P(B=M\mid \mathrm{two},E)=0.01.
\]

Then

\[
V_E(\mathrm{one})=\$990{,}000,
\]

while

\[
V_E(\mathrm{two})=\$11{,}000.
\]

EDT recommends one-boxing.

These conditional calibration assumptions are stronger than merely saying that the predictor is “99% accurate” overall. Aggregate accuracy alone does not determine every conditional probability needed for the calculation.

The disagreement is genuine. CDT does not deny that one-boxers tend to receive more money. It denies that changing the present action causally changes the earlier prediction.

Nor is temporal order the entire issue. An earlier event can influence a later predictor. What matters is the assumed causal structure, not simply whether a variable occurs before or after another.

Newcomb’s problem therefore tests whether rational choice should track causal improvement holding background conditions fixed, or the favorable outcomes associated with being the kind of agent who chooses differently. The reference reports continuing disagreement; the formal calculation does not settle that normative dispute.

## 5. Actions, policies, and strategic dependence

A policy specifies actions across possible information states. Write

\[
\pi_t:H_t\to\mathcal A_t,
\]

where \(H_t\) is the history available at time \(t\). An intervention-based policy evaluation is

\[
V_C(\pi\mid E)
=
\mathbb E\!\left[
u(O)\mid
\operatorname{do}\bigl(A_t=\pi_t(H_t)\text{ for all }t\bigr),E
\right].
\]

This extends the previous operational interpretation. Its application requires care about what the agent can actually choose.

In Newcomb’s problem, installing a binding one-boxing device **before** the predictor examines the player can causally affect the prediction. CDT may recommend that commitment. Once the prediction is fixed, however, an available act of taking both boxes still has the additional causal payoff.

Thus these are different decision problems:

- choosing an executable commitment before prediction;
- choosing an action after prediction;
- merely imagining that one’s decision procedure had always been different.

The third is not automatically an intervention available to the present agent. A policy’s benefits must follow through a specified causal pathway, rather than being imported from a favorable comparison class.

Similar distinctions arise in games. Consider a prisoner’s dilemma with your payoffs

\[
u(C,C)=3,\quad u(D,C)=5,\quad
u(C,D)=0,\quad u(D,D)=1.
\]

If your intervention cannot change the other player’s action, and \(q\) is your current probability that they cooperate, then

\[
V_C(C)=3q,
\qquad
V_C(D)=5q+(1-q)=1+4q.
\]

Defection wins by \(1+q\).

This remains the causal recommendation if the other player is a highly correlated “twin,” provided correlation does not become causal influence. EDT can instead favor cooperation when your cooperation strongly indicates theirs.

But if your action signals intentions, affects future cooperation, or implements an enforceable agreement, those are causal consequences. CDT does not generally prescribe defection.

For a one-shot randomized action with an independent randomizer and fixed intervention model,

\[
V_C(\mu)=\sum_a\mu(a)V_C(a).
\]

Randomization cannot exceed the best pure action’s value. It can matter when adopting a randomized policy itself changes others’ responses or what they can predict.

## 6. Self-knowledge and instability

The most difficult criticisms concern actions that provide evidence about the agent’s own causal situation.

### The psychopath button

The reference describes Paul, who considers pressing a button that kills all psychopaths. He values their disappearance but strongly prefers living with psychopaths to dying. He also believes that pressing is strong evidence that he is a psychopath.

Let

\[
p=P(T=1\mid E),
\]

where \(T=1\) means Paul is a psychopath. Suppose pressing produces benefit \(b>0\) if he is not a psychopath and loss \(-L\) if he is. Normalize not pressing to zero. Then simple CDT gives

\[
V_C(\mathrm{press})=(1-p)b-pL.
\]

It recommends pressing when

\[
p<\frac{b}{b+L}.
\]

Yet Paul might also believe

\[
P(T=1\mid A=\mathrm{press},E)\approx 1.
\]

His contemplated action seems to reveal exactly the danger that the intervention calculation discounts.

The criticism is not that CDT always presses regardless of beliefs or costs. Rather, it can recommend pressing under a low prior probability of psychopathy even when pressing would be powerful evidence of psychopathy.

The reference reports Joyce’s response that CDT can use information about an agent’s inclinations as evidence. If Paul actually notices a disturbing enthusiasm for pressing, that observation can raise \(p\) before the alternatives are evaluated.

This is a reasonable operational distinction:

> Update on actual evidence about deliberation; do not automatically update on every action merely because it is hypothetically considered.

Nevertheless, it leaves an important boundary unsettled. When deliberation generates a tentative decision, how much evidential force should that decision have? Treating it as evidence may change the preferred action, which changes the evidence again.

### Prediction and changing recommendations

The article’s Death in Damascus scenario illustrates this instability. Death predicts which city the agent will visit and waits there.

Let \(q\) be the agent’s current probability that Death awaits in Damascus. Holding Death’s location fixed under intervention,

\[
P(\text{survival}\mid\operatorname{do}(\mathrm{Damascus}))=1-q,
\]

\[
P(\text{survival}\mid\operatorname{do}(\mathrm{other\ city}))=q.
\]

The agent prefers whichever city currently seems less likely to contain Death. But anticipating that choice may shift beliefs about Death’s prediction and reverse the recommendation.

It is misleading to say CDT cannot represent this correlation. A model can represent it explicitly. The problem is finding a stable relationship between predictive beliefs, deliberation, and the eventual choice.

### The adversarial offer

The reference also presents two boxes, each costing \$1. Yesterday, a seller placed \$3 in every box she predicted the buyer would not acquire. The buyer may acquire one box or neither.

At least one box contains \$3. Consequently, if

\[
v_i=\mathbb E[\text{contents of box }i\mid E],
\]

then

\[
v_1+v_2\ge 3,
\qquad
\max(v_1,v_2)\ge 1.50.
\]

Holding the contents fixed, simple CDT therefore finds some purchase with positive expected net value.

Yet under the intended assumption that the seller correctly predicts the purchased box with probability \(0.75\), the acquired box is empty with that probability. The buyer’s average net payoff is

\[
0.25(3)-1=-\$0.25.
\]

The tension concerns holding initial beliefs fixed while selecting an action correlated with unfavorable contents. Predictor accuracy and available randomization must be specified consistently; a genuinely unpredictable private randomizer may undermine the assumed accuracy.

These examples motivate the article’s reported criticisms concerning voluntary losses and diachronic Dutch books. They challenge how causal evaluation interacts with learning and repeated deliberation, rather than disproving expected utility maximization by itself.

## 7. Applying CDT responsibly

For an unfamiliar problem, a workable procedure is:

1. **Specify the decision time and available interventions.** Distinguish acting now from committing earlier.
2. **Define utilities over complete outcomes.** Include action costs and effects on others.
3. **Separate actual evidence from hypothetical action selection.**
4. **Model dependencies.** Identify common causes, causal pathways, and purely predictive associations.
5. **Evaluate each intervention using the same current background beliefs**, while recomputing its downstream effects.
6. **Check sensitivity and stability.** Would plausible causal models reverse the ranking? Would learning one’s inclination change it?

The first five steps operationalize a standard causal reading. The sixth is a diagnostic addition: it exposes uncertainty rather than pretending CDT supplies a universally accepted solution to self-predictive cases.

## Conclusion

CDT evaluates actions by what they would cause, not merely by what they would reveal. Its central discipline is to preserve justified background beliefs while varying the action and tracing its consequences. This is powerful in ordinary confounded decisions. Prediction and self-knowledge expose its harder questions: which counterfactual intervention is appropriate, what deliberation teaches, and whether locally attractive causal choices form a stable policy.
